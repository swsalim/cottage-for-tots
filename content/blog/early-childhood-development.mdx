A milestone is a skill most children can do by a given age. The CDC sets that bar at 75% or more, and it sorts the skills into how a child plays, learns, speaks, acts, and moves. This page is that map from 2 months through age 5, plus the play that fits each stage.

Age-by-age toy choices live on their own pages: <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-infants">the first year</Link>, <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-1-year-olds">1-year-olds</Link>, <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-2-year-olds">2-year-olds</Link>, <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-3-year-olds">3-year-olds</Link>, and <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-4-year-olds">4-year-olds</Link>. The large-muscle and small-hand split is in <Link href="/blog/gross-fine-motor-skills">gross and fine motor skills</Link>.

**Quick answer:** Use the CDC checklist for your child's age. A missed milestone, a skill that disappeared, or a worry you cannot shake is a reason to talk with the doctor and ask about developmental screening. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that screening at 9, 18, and 30 months, and autism screening at 18 and 24 months. Between visits, play on the floor, talk during care, and read a book. You will not spoil a baby by holding them.

## What a milestone is, and what it is not

The [CDC milestone index](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html), reviewed 17 February 2026, says these checklists are not a substitute for a standardized screening tool. Missing one item does not diagnose a delay. Meeting every item does not rule a concern out. You know your child best.

The checklists also adjust for prematurity in the [Milestone Tracker app](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones-app/). A baby born early is compared with their corrected age, not the date on the calendar, until the doctor says otherwise.

CDC's page on [monitoring and screening](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/about/developmental-monitoring-and-screening.html), reviewed the same day, separates two jobs. Monitoring is what you do at home: watch play, speech, actions, and movement, and bring notes to the well-child visit. Screening is a formal questionnaire, done by someone trained to use it.

## Milestones most children reach

Each row is a sample from that age's CDC page, reviewed in May 2026. Open the linked page for the full list. The play column is a place to start, using what you already have.

| Age | A few things most children do | Play that fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [2 months](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-months.html) | Smiles when you smile. Watches you move. Holds head up during tummy time. Makes sounds other than crying. | Your face, your voice, and a short tummy-time session with a toy at eye level. Stay beside them. |
| [6 months](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/6-months.html) | Laughs. Takes turns making sounds. Reaches for a toy. Rolls from tummy to back. | Floor play. Put a toy just out of reach and let them work toward it. Copy their sounds. |
| [1 year](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html) | Waves bye-bye. Puts a block in a cup. Pulls up to stand. Walks while holding furniture. Picks up small bits of food with thumb and finger. | A container and one block. A push across the floor once standing is steady. Baby walkers are not recommended. |
| [2 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html) | Says two words together, such as "more milk." Runs. Kicks a ball. Puts toy food on a toy plate. Notices when someone is hurt or upset. | A ball to kick. Blocks to stack and knock down. One hat or spoon for simple pretend. |
| [3 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html) | Holds a short back-and-forth conversation. Joins other children to play. Strings large beads. Puts on loose clothing. Uses a fork. | Large beads, playdough, and time with other children. Give words for feelings. |
| [4 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html) | Pretends to be someone else. Says sentences of four or more words. Catches a large ball most of the time. Holds a crayon with fingers and thumb, not a fist. | Dress-up, a large ball, and a story where you ask what happens next. |
| [5 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html) | Takes turns in a game. Tells a story with at least two events. Counts to 10. Hops on one foot. Buttons some buttons. | A simple turn-taking game, a chore they can finish, and time to tell you about the day. |

The 5-year page adds one detail that is easy to miss: attention of 5 to 10 minutes during a story or an art activity counts. Screen time does not.

## What to do between check-ups

The same CDC pages include the everyday version of help. It is short, and it repeats.

**Answer the sound.** At 2 months, smile and talk back when the baby makes a sound. Talk, read, and sing during feeding, dressing, and bathing. The 2-month page says you will not spoil a baby by holding them or responding to them.

**Get on the floor.** At 6 months, tummy time and a toy to roll toward are the movement practice. A <Link href="/blog/baby-play-mat">play mat</Link> gives that a clear spot. At 1 year, blocks, a cup, and a safe push toy match the hands and the walking. Pots and pans count as a first instrument.

**Name what they are doing.** At 2 years, if they say "or nana," you say "You want more banana." Point to pictures in a book. <Link href="/blog/reading-aloud-language-development">Reading aloud</Link> is one book, one new word, and one question.

**Follow their play.** At 3 and 4, the CDC suggests role play before a new place, playdough for the hands, and outdoor time where the child chooses the game. Pretend with household props is in <Link href="/blog/pretend-play-social-skills">pretend play for 3-year-olds</Link>. A hard feeling, and what to do in the moment, is in <Link href="/blog/emotional-regulation-activities">emotional regulation activities</Link>.

**Keep a routine.** The 2-year page suggests a calm bedtime: pajamas, teeth, one or two books. Sleep ranges on those pages are 12 to 16 hours a day at 4 to 12 months, including naps, and 11 to 14 hours at age 2, including naps. Offer the routine. Let the doctor sort out a sleep problem.

Food belongs in the same visits. The 2-month page says breast milk or formula only for about the first 6 months. The 2-year page says you offer the food and the child decides how much. This page does not replace feeding advice from the child's doctor.

## When to ask for screening

Ask at the next visit, or sooner, if any of these are true:

- One or more milestones for the age are missing.
- A skill the child used to have is gone.
- Something else worries you, even if you cannot name the milestone.

Say what you see. Ask for developmental screening. If you and the doctor are still concerned, the CDC says to ask for a referral to a specialist and to call your state or territory's early intervention program. The directory is at [cdc.gov/FindEI](https://www.cdc.gov/FindEI). Early intervention programs usually serve children from birth to age 3. From age 3, the local public school system is the usual next call. Families can refer themselves. You do not have to wait for a referral to ask.

The AAP ages for routine screening, quoted on the CDC index, are 9, 18, and 30 months for general development, and 18 and 24 months for autism. If a visit was missed, ask for the screening at the next one. A concern at any other age is also a reason to ask.

CDC reports that developmental disabilities are common. Citing a 2026 *Pediatrics* analysis, its monitoring page says about 1 in 5 children ages 3 to 17 has one or more developmental disabilities. That figure is a reason to check, not a prediction about your child.

## What to skip

Skip comparing your child with a cousin, a classmate, or a video. The checklist already says "most children," which means some children do a skill later and are fine, and some need help earlier than a birthday.

Skip a pile of new toys as a substitute for the floor time above. Healey and Mendelsohn, in [Selecting Appropriate Toys for Young Children in the Digital Era](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30509931/) (*Pediatrics*, 2019), say the evidence that toys help comes mainly from play with a caregiver.

Skip treating age 2 as a behavior problem to fix. The 2-year checklist is about noticing other people's feelings, two-word phrases, running, and simple pretend. If those are hard, or if daily life is hard, ask the doctor. A label does not help you watch the skills.

Screen time limits for these ages are in <Link href="/blog/balancing-screen-time">balancing screen time with play</Link>. The CDC tip sheets still tell families to keep screens to video calls before age 2, and to no more than 1 hour a day of a children's program with an adult from ages 2 to 4.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is early childhood development?

It is how a child plays, learns, speaks, acts, and moves from birth through about age 5. The CDC publishes free checklists at 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 24, and 30 months, and at 3, 4, and 5 years. A milestone on those lists is something at least 75% of children can do by that age.

### Do I need the full checklist?

Yes, if you want the complete picture. This page shows a few items from each stage so you can see the shape of the years. The CDC checklist, the app, or the printable PDF is the list to take to the doctor.

### What if my child misses one milestone?

Talk with the doctor and ask about developmental screening. One missed item is not a diagnosis. Lost skills, or a worry that will not go away, are also reasons to call. You do not need to wait for the next birthday.

### When is developmental screening done?

The AAP recommends general developmental screening at 9, 18, and 30 months, and autism screening at 18 and 24 months, during regular well-child visits. Ask again any time you are concerned, and ask if a recommended visit was skipped.

### What play actually helps?

The play on the CDC tip sheets: responding to sounds, tummy time, books, blocks, balls, pretend, and time with other children once they are old enough to join. A caregiver who is in the play matters more than a special toy.

## What to try first

Open the CDC page for your child's age. Mark what you have seen this month. Bring that note to the next visit, including anything that worries you. Tonight, get on the floor for ten minutes and do the one activity in the table that matches the age you have now.
