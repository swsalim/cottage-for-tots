Children’s riding toys are the ones a child sits on, stands on, or straddles and moves: a foot-to-floor ride-on, a rocking ride-on, a balance bike, a tricycle, a scooter, or a pedal bike. This page is the order those types tend to fit, from about 12 months through about age 6, plus fit, helmets, and where the ride should happen.

The wider toddler shelf, from push toys and balls to tunnels and low climbers, is in <Link href="/blog/gross-motor-toys-toddlers">gross motor toys for toddlers</Link>. Floor games that do not need a riding toy are in <Link href="/blog/indoor-gross-motor-activities-toddlers">indoor gross motor activities for toddlers</Link>. What most children are practicing in each year, beyond riding, is in the age guides: <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-1-year-olds">about 12 to 24 months</Link>, <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-2-year-olds">about 24 to 36 months</Link>, <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-3-year-olds">about age 3</Link>, and <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-4-year-olds">about age 4</Link>.

**Quick answer:** Wait until walking is somewhat steady. Start with a low ride-on the child pushes with both feet flat on the floor, often with four wheels. A small rocking ride-on can share that stage if the feet reach the floor or the base and the rock is short. A tricycle is the pedal toy most children are ready for around age 3. A three-wheel scooter comes before a two-wheel scooter. A two-wheeled pedal bike is a later fit, about age 5 or 6, and only in the size the child fits today. Use a real helmet on scooters and other riding toys every time. Keep the ride away from stairs, pools, driveways, and streets.

## What has to be true before any ride-on

CPSC staff’s [2020 age-determination guidelines](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/2020%20Age%20Determination%20Guidelines%20FINAL.pdf) say children under 1 cannot use ride-on toys, because those toys need a sense of balance that usually shows up when a child walks with some steadiness. The same document says children around 12 months begin to enjoy recreational play, including ride-ons. Those guidelines are staff views to help judge whether a toy fits a child’s skills and play. They are not a mandatory rule, the Commission has not reviewed them, and they are not a medical chart.

The CDC’s [Learn the Signs. Act Early.](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html) checklists say what most children, 75% or more, do by a given age. They are not a shopping list, and each age page says they are not a substitute for a validated screening tool. By [12 months](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html), most children pull up to stand and walk while holding furniture. By [15 months](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/15-months.html), most take a few steps on their own. By [18 months](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html), most walk without holding on to anyone or anything. By [2 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html), most run, kick a ball, and walk up a few stairs. How those movements differ from hand skills is in <Link href="/blog/gross-fine-motor-skills">gross versus fine motor skills</Link>.

A child who is still only cruising along the couch is in the push-toy stage, not the ride-on stage. A push toy they walk behind is a different object. That choice is in the gross-motor toy guide. The CDC’s [12-month tips](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html) also say baby walkers are not recommended. A baby walker is a seat on wheels. It is not a foot-to-floor ride-on. [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-home/Pages/baby-walkers-a-dangerous-choice.aspx) says walkers do not teach walking, can delay walking, and can send a child down stairs, toward a burn, or into a pool faster than an adult can stop them. The AAP has called for a ban on wheeled baby walkers. The 1-year guide already lists them under what to skip.

If your child is not doing a movement you expected, or has lost a skill, the CDC says to talk with your child’s doctor and ask about developmental screening.

## Ride-on toys by age

Read the label on the toy you are holding, then use this table as the staff-and-pediatric picture of the skill, not as a birthday deadline. NAEYC’s [toys by age](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/topics/play/toys) says each child develops at their own pace, and that a safe toy from another age list can still be a good fit.

| Age | What the body is doing | Riding type that matches | Leave it for later |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| About 12 to 18 months, after walking is steadier | Pushes with both feet together. Steering is still hard. Cannot pedal. | A low foot-to-floor ride-on with four or more wheels. A small rocking ride-on with a short arc. | Pedals, two wheels, and a sit-inside toy that is hard to steer |
| About 19 to 23 months | Walking is more stable. A sit-inside foot-powered toy is harder to steer than a straddle. | The same foot-to-floor ride-on. A wagon they can pull. The same small rocking ride-on. | Pedals they have not learned, and any motorized vehicle |
| About 2 years | More balance. Can bounce in the seat. Some children start to pedal late in this year and have not mastered it. | A slow three-wheel scooter with a wide standing platform, if they can stand and steer it. | A tricycle as the usual choice. NAEYC says tricycles are probably a better fit from about 3. |
| About 3 years | Can pedal, and can use a steering wheel or handlebars. | A low tricycle sized to the child. A three-wheel scooter. | A two-wheel scooter or a bicycle. The balance for those is not there yet. |
| About 4 to 5 years | Interest shifts toward the bikes and scooters older children use. By about 5, many have the balance for two wheels, and it depends on practice. | A two-wheel scooter or a pedal bike only if the fit checks below are true. | The street. Children this age do not understand the risk of riding where cars are. |
| About 6 years | Most have the physical ability to ride a bicycle without training wheels, and the coordination for hand brakes. | A pedal bike that fits today, with a helmet, off the street, with you there. | Riding in traffic because the birthday arrived. |

### Foot-to-floor and push-along ride-ons

In this guide, a foot-to-floor ride-on and a push-along ride-on are the same idea: the child sits or straddles, and the feet on the floor are what move it. CPSC staff say children can use a straddled, foot-powered ride-on only after they walk with some steadiness. In the 12- to 18-month band, especially the younger part of it, they may not alternate their feet. They push with both feet at once. They cannot pedal.

Staff say four or more wheels tend to be more stable than three or two. The wheels should sit wide enough to be stable, and not so wide that the child cannot swing a leg over. The toy should be low, so getting on and off is easy, and both feet should be flat on the floor when the child is seated. Recessed wheels keep the feet and legs from banging the wheels. For the youngest riders, staff say casters can help, because the toy can move in any direction without tipping. The first ride-on does not need a steering mechanism. Young children may not be able to use one.

A sit-inside ride-on that the child still powers with their feet shows up in the staff notes for about 19 to 23 months. Staff say it is harder to steer and maneuver than the straddle type. A wagon fits that same band. Bright colors, a horn, and a bin for carrying toys are features staff say appeal then. They are extras. Feet on the floor, a stable base, and you beside the path are the part that matters.

### Rocking ride-ons

CPSC staff say a simple rocking horse may be introduced in the same 12- to 18-month band. A suitable one is small and easy to get on and off, so a fall is less likely. The child’s feet should touch the floor or the base while they are seated. The rocking arc should be confined, meaning the toy cannot pitch far. Staff repeat those same limits for 19 to 23 months. A high seat, dangling feet, or a long rock fails that description.

### Three-wheel scooters, then two-wheel scooters

CPSC staff say that at about 2 years, children have more coordination and balance, and slow-moving three-wheeled scooters with wide standing platforms become attractive. At about 3, staff say children can use three-wheeled scooters, and they have not developed the balance to operate two-wheeled scooters or bicycles. At about 4 and 5, staff say most children, by age 5 and depending on experience, have the balance and coordination for two-wheeled scooters and bicycles without training wheels. The same passage says children this age do not understand the risks of riding where cars are, and that adult supervision is required. Protective equipment for those rides, in the staff text, includes helmets and knee and elbow pads.

A CPSC holiday reminder from [November 2017](https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2018/Dont-Play-with-Toy-Safety-CPSC-Tips-for-Safe-Gifts) says supervision is key with scooters and other riding toys, that helmets should be worn properly and sized to fit, and that a scooter should not be ridden on a street or roadway with motor vehicles. In the 2016 figures summarized there, riding toys, specifically non-motorized scooters, were the toy category associated with the most emergency-department-treated injuries to children younger than 15. Every riding-toy death in that report involved a motor vehicle.

### When a pedal bike fits

[HealthyChildren.org’s tricycle page](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/Ready-For-A-Tricycle.aspx), adapted from the AAP’s *Caring for Your Baby and Young Child* and updated 4 August 2021, says not to get a tricycle until the child can handle it, and that most children are ready around age 3. NAEYC lists ride-on equipment for 2-year-olds and says tricycles are probably not the fit until children are 3. For 3- to 6-year-olds, NAEYC includes tricycles with the other ride-on equipment, along with wagons. CPSC staff are a little earlier on the first tries: some children may start a pedal tricycle in the latter half of the 2-year group, and they have not mastered pedaling. At age 3, staff say children have developed the ability to pedal and the coordination to use a steering wheel or handlebars, and that they enjoy tricycles and four-wheeled pedal vehicles. Staff say the tricycle should be sized to the child, and that 12- or 13-inch wheels are about the right size for this age group.

The AAP’s look-for list on that tricycle page is concrete. Choose one built low, with big wheels, because it is less likely to tip. Use closed-toe shoes. Ride only in protected places, not near automobiles, driveways, or swimming pools. A child on a tricycle sits low enough that a driver who is backing up may not see them.

CPSC staff say a 3-year-old can use a small bicycle with training wheels, and that a foot brake is the one that fits, because children this age cannot yet use hand brakes. They also say the balance for a bicycle without training wheels is not there at 3. [HealthyChildren.org’s bike-fit page](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/choosing-the-right-size-bicycle.aspx), updated 11 August 2022, says not to push a child to ride a two-wheeled bike until they are ready, at about age 5 or 6, and to buy the size that fits now. A bike the child has to grow into is described there as especially dangerous. Staff say most children by age 6 have the physical ability to ride without training wheels, can use hand brakes, and need a bicycle they can stand over with both feet on the ground. Staff also say 6-year-olds have developed some understanding of what can happen when they share space with cars and pedestrians. That is not an age for the street. The AAP’s [first-bike page](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/your-childs-first-tricycle-or-bike-teaching-them-to-ride-safely.aspx) says young children should ride only with adult supervision and off the street. The decision to allow an older child into the street depends on traffic, maturity, and whether they can follow the rules of the road.

Motorized ride-ons are outside this progression. CPSC staff say most 2-year-olds lack the steering skill to keep a slow-moving motorized vehicle away from hazards, even when they can turn a wheel. This page stays with toys the child powers.

## Balance bike or tricycle

A tricycle and a balance bike ask for different work.

A tricycle keeps three wheels under the child. The new skills are pedaling and steering. The age notes above are the ones to use: most children around age 3 for the AAP, probably not until 3 for NAEYC, with CPSC staff describing early tries late in the 2-year year and a real pedal-and-steer fit at 3. Look for the low frame and big wheels on the AAP page, a helmet, closed-toe shoes, and a protected place.

A balance bike has no pedals. The child sits and pushes with their feet, and they have to hold two wheels upright while they move. CPSC staff do not name balance bikes in the 2020 ride-on chapter. They do say that at age 3, children have not developed the balance for two-wheeled scooters and bicycles. A balance bike is still a two-wheeled toy. Treat the fit like the other foot-powered toys: both feet need to reach the ground, and the path rules below still apply. It is not a reason to skip the helmet.

The useful comparison in the research is balance bike versus training wheels, not balance bike versus tricycle. [Blommenstein and van der Kamp](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9310799/), in the *British Journal of Developmental Psychology* in 2022, asked parents of 4- to 6-year-olds in Dutch cities about how their children learned to ride. Of 173 questionnaires, 7 were incomplete. Among children who could already pedal a regular bike without side wheels or a parent holding on, the balance-bike group (n = 52) had a mean age of independent cycling of 3.5 years (standard deviation 0.6). The training-wheels group (n = 44) had a mean of 4.5 years (standard deviation 0.9). The balance-bike group had also started practice younger, at a mean of 2.1 years versus 3.2 years. After the authors accounted for that earlier start, the balance-bike group was still earlier by about half a year in their regression. Parents’ education was not the same across groups: about 90% of the balance-bike parents had tertiary education, compared with about 48% of the training-wheels parents. This was a parent questionnaire, not a study that assigned the bikes. The authors say further research is needed. It does not show that a balance bike is safer than a tricycle, and it does not give a minimum age.

Use the two toys for the jobs they actually do. A tricycle is the pedal toy for the year around age 3, on a protected path. A balance bike is one way to practice staying upright before pedals, if the child’s feet reach and you can stay with them. AAP’s advice still stands for the two-wheeled pedal bike: wait until the child is ready, about age 5 or 6, and buy the size that fits that day.

## Fit checks

[HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/choosing-the-right-size-bicycle.aspx) says to bring the child when you look at a bike, because a fit you can see matters more than a surprise. On any style of bike, the seated child, hands on the handlebar, must be able to place the balls of both feet on the ground. Standing over the center bar, both feet flat, there should be about a 1-inch gap between the crotch and the bar. If the bike has hand brakes, the child has to grasp them and press hard enough to stop. CPSC staff add that an appropriately sized bike in the 6- to 8-year description lets the child stand and straddle with both feet on the ground, and that hand brakes match the coordination they describe at about age 6, not at age 3.

| Check | A fit | Set it aside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feet, on a foot-powered toy | Both feet flat on the floor while seated | Toes only, or a seat they are meant to grow into |
| Width and wheels | Four or more wheels for a new walker, wide enough to be stable, narrow enough to swing a leg over. Recessed wheels so the feet do not hit them | Two wheels, or a stance so wide the leg cannot clear the seat |
| Rocking ride-on | Small, easy to climb on, feet on the floor or the base, a short rocking arc | A high seat, dangling feet, or a long rock |
| Tricycle | Low, with big wheels, sized to the child. Staff describe 12- or 13-inch wheels around age 3 | A tall frame that tips, or pedals the child cannot turn |
| Pedal bike | The AAP seated-feet test and the 1-inch straddle gap. A foot brake if you are looking at the training-wheel option staff describe at age 3 | A bike bought large on purpose. Hand brakes the child cannot work |
| Shoes | Closed-toe shoes for a tricycle or a bike | Bare toes beside a wheel or a pedal |

Ride-on toys also have to meet stability requirements and must not collapse in an overload test. That is the maker’s duty under the toy standard, which CPSC summarizes in [section 4.15 of its toy-safety guidance](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Toy-Safety). Your check is the label, the fit in the table, wheels and axles that are still intact, and a path you can see.

## Helmets

CPSC’s [toy tips](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Toys) say to get safety gear, including helmets, for scooters and other riding toys, and to use that gear every time. That includes a short ride and an indoor path. The AAP tricycle page says to teach the helmet from the start, on every ride. The label should say the helmet meets CPSC standards. The chin strap should fit snugly, and the helmet should not move much over the forehead when it is strapped.

The [bike-fit page](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/choosing-the-right-size-bicycle.aspx) adds the rest of the fit. The helmet sits squarely on the head and covers the top of the forehead. It sits parallel to the ground when the head is upright. It fits when it does not slide over the eyes if you push it, pull it, or the child shakes their head. The AAP’s [first-bike page](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/your-childs-first-tricycle-or-bike-teaching-them-to-ride-safely.aspx) says to start that habit with the first bike or tricycle. The same bike-fit page says helmet use decreases head injuries and deaths related to bicycle crashes with motor vehicles.

A costume helmet is not that gear. Section 4.19 of the toy standard says simulated protective devices, including toy helmets, have to be labeled to show they are not safety protective devices. Use the helmet that meets the CPSC bicycle standard. Leave the dress-up helmet off the ride. Take the real helmet off when the ride is over, before sleep. For the two-wheel scooter and bike years, CPSC staff also name knee and elbow pads.

## Where to ride

The place is part of the toy.

- **Stairs.** [HealthyChildren.org’s safety notes for ages 2 to 4](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Safety-for-Your-Child-2-to-4-Years.aspx) say a child this age can fall down stairs, and to use gates on stairways. A ride-on does not belong on a path that ends at those stairs, or in a room that opens onto them. The indoor activities guide uses a push across a rug when that is the only route.
- **Streets and driveways.** The same 2-to-4 page says not to let a child play or ride a tricycle in the street, and that driveways are dangerous. Walk behind the car before you back out. The tricycle page says not to ride near automobiles or driveways, because the rider is low and a backing driver may not see them. The [4-year checkup list](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/Your-Childs-Checkups/Pages/your-checkup-checklist-4-years-old.aspx) says a 4-year-old is not ready to cross the street alone and still needs an adult during outside play.
- **Pools.** The tricycle page says not to ride near swimming pools. The 4-year checkup list says a 4-year-old still needs an adult around water.
- **Scooter roads.** CPSC’s 2017 reminder says to avoid riding a scooter on a street or roadway with motor vehicles.
- **Dusk and dark.** The AAP’s first-bike page says never to ride at dusk or in the dark, for children and for adults.

An apartment ride is the same list in a smaller space. You need a flat path, feet that reach, and a route that does not lead to stairs, a pool, a driveway, or a street. If the hallway is the whole route and it ends at a stair gate, skip the ride-on. The gate is there because children fall down stairs. It is not a plan for a wheeled toy. Use the push-across-a-rug ideas in <Link href="/blog/indoor-gross-motor-activities-toddlers">indoor gross motor activities</Link>. Stay in the room either way. Helmets still go on for a scooter or other riding toy, including indoors.

## What the ride practices

CPSC staff say ride-on toys matter for a sense of balance, physical fitness, and coordination. They also say children like the movement and the pretend of copying adults they know. Around age 2, staff say pretend play is underway, and realistic vehicles, such as a pretend fire engine, are popular with children and with parents buying them. The AAP tricycle page says a tricycle gives fun and exercise, and new hazards with it.

That is the skill list this page will stand behind: balance, coordination, a chance to move, and a little pretend, with you there. A ride-on does not assign a milestone. The CDC ages for walking and running are the reference for the body skill underneath the toy. One session does not have to fill the day. A few minutes on a clear path, more than once, matches the way the gross-motor guide treats these toys. The indoor guide is where the day gets split into short bursts when you are not riding.

## Riding toy ideas by type

These are types, not products. No brand, price, or rating belongs in this list. The product column is empty on purpose. Use it later as a slot for one pick, after the fit and the path are settled.

| Toy type | What the child does | When it tends to fit | Product pick |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Foot-to-floor ride-on, four or more wheels | Sits or straddles and pushes with both feet flat on the floor | After walking is steadier, often in the 12- to 18-month band. Low seat. No pedals required | None yet |
| Sit-inside foot-powered ride-on | Pushes with the feet from a seat they sit down into | About 19 to 23 months. Harder to steer than a straddle, so the path has to be simple | None yet |
| Low rocking ride-on | Rocks a short distance with the feet on the floor or the base | About 12 to 23 months, if it is small and easy to climb on | None yet |
| Wagon | Pulls a low wagon, or rides in one you pull | Staff list wagons for about 19 to 23 months, and NAEYC includes them for ages 3 to 6 | None yet |
| Balance bike | Sits and pushes a pedal-less two-wheeler, and has to stay upright | Feet reach the ground. It is not the age-3 pedal toy. The research above is about learning a later pedal bike | None yet |
| Low tricycle | Pedals and steers, with three wheels holding them up | Most children around age 3. Low frame, big wheels, closed-toe shoes | None yet |
| Three-wheel scooter | Stands on a wide platform and pushes | Staff: attractive around age 2 if it is slow, and usable at about 3 | None yet |
| Two-wheel scooter | Stands and balances on two wheels | Not at age 3. Most children, by about 5 and depending on practice, in the staff description. Pads and a helmet | None yet |
| Pedal bike | Pedals a two-wheeler that fits today | About age 5 or 6, when the child is ready. Training wheels and a foot brake are the earlier bicycle option staff describe at age 3 | None yet |

One type that matches the child in front of you is enough. A second riding toy does not add a skill the first one skipped.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are children’s riding toys?

Toys a child moves by sitting, standing, or straddling: foot-to-floor ride-ons, rocking ride-ons, balance bikes, tricycles, scooters, wagons, and pedal bikes. A push toy they walk behind is a different object. That one is in the gross-motor toy guide. A baby walker, which is a seat on wheels, is a product the AAP says to throw out.

### Which ride-on toys fit toddlers?

After walking is somewhat steady, a low foot-to-floor ride-on with four or more wheels, and both feet flat on the floor. A small rocking ride-on with a short arc can fit the same ages if the feet reach. Pedal tricycles are a later toy. NAEYC says they are probably a better fit from about age 3, and the AAP says most children are ready around age 3.

### How do ride-on toys change by age?

Foot-powered and rocking toys come first, from about 12 months once walking is steadier, through about age 2. Three-wheel scooters enter around age 2 to 3. Tricycles are the usual pedal toy around age 3. Two-wheel scooters and two-wheel pedal bikes wait until about age 5 or 6, and only if the child can actually balance and the bike fits. The table higher on this page is the full pass. CPSC staff ages are not a mandatory rule.

### Is a balance bike better than a tricycle?

They are different toys. A tricycle is for pedaling and steering while three wheels hold the child up, usually around age 3. A balance bike has no pedals and asks the child to balance on two wheels. A 2022 parent study in the Netherlands found that children who had practiced on a balance bike reported independent pedaling younger than children who had used training wheels, including after the authors accounted for an earlier start. That study did not compare balance bikes with tricycles, and it did not assign the bikes. Use a tricycle for the pedal year. Use a balance bike only when the feet reach and you can supervise.

### When can a child use a two-wheel scooter?

CPSC staff say three-wheel scooters with a wide platform are the earlier scooter, around age 2 to 3, and that 3-year-olds do not yet have the balance for a two-wheel scooter. Most children, by about age 5 and depending on experience, do, in that staff description. Add a helmet every time, and knee and elbow pads in the staff note for ages 4 and 5. Keep the scooter off streets where cars drive.

### When is a child ready for a pedal bike?

For a tricycle, around age 3 if they can pedal and the trike is low, stable, and sized to them. For a two-wheeled pedal bike, the AAP says not to push it until the child is ready, at about age 5 or 6, and to buy the size that fits now. CPSC staff say a small bike with training wheels and a foot brake can be used at age 3, and that hand brakes and riding without training wheels fit later, around age 6 for most children. Young children ride off the street, with an adult.

### Does a riding toy need a helmet indoors?

CPSC says to use helmets for scooters and other riding toys every time. A costume helmet does not count. The helmet should meet the CPSC bicycle standard, sit level over the forehead, and stay put when the child shakes their head. The chin strap should be snug.

### Can children ride these toys in an apartment?

Only on a flat path that does not lead to stairs, and only while you are there. If the hallway ends at a stair gate, use the indoor activities guide instead. Driveways, streets, and the area around a pool are out, including on the way from the door to a park. Helmets stay on.

### How is this different from the gross-motor toy guide?

That guide chooses several movement toys for about 12 to 36 months. Ride-ons are one short section there. This page is only riding toys, and it follows them through about age 6: foot-to-floor, rocking, balance bike, tricycle, three-wheel scooter, two-wheel scooter, and pedal bike. The age guides still cover the rest of the shelf for each year.

## What to try first

Watch one walk across the room. If the child still needs a hand or the couch, stay with a push toy and the indoor games. If they are walking and want to move a seat with their feet, look for one low foot-to-floor ride-on, four wheels, feet flat, and a path with no stairs at the end. Put a real helmet on before the first push.

If they are closer to 3 and already pedaling in the air when they sit, use the tricycle checks instead: low, big wheels, closed-toe shoes, helmet, and a place with no cars and no pool. Leave the two-wheeled bike until the fit page’s age and the foot-on-the-ground tests are both true.
