A playset is a small scene a child can move: a house, a bus, a market, a few figures, maybe a pot or a bed. It teaches a place when you add one true detail and then let the child play. It teaches a stereotype when one costume is asked to stand for a whole country.

This page is that setup for about ages 3 to 6. Household pretend, with a pot and a doll, is in <Link href="/blog/pretend-play-social-skills">pretend play for 3-year-olds</Link>. Reading one book beside the scene is in <Link href="/blog/reading-aloud-language-development">reading aloud</Link>. Sharing one set with another child is in <Link href="/blog/cooperative-play-toys-sharing">cooperative play</Link>.

**Quick answer:** Start with a scene the child already understands, such as eating or going to bed. Add one book or one photo of real people in the place you are playing. Use figures too large to swallow. Say one true thing, then follow their story. Do not say "this is how everyone there lives."

## What the child is ready to do

The CDC's [3-year milestone page](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html), reviewed in May 2026, says most children this age notice other children and join them to play. They can also talk about what is happening in a picture: running, eating, playing. The [4-year page](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html) says most children pretend to be someone else, such as a teacher or a dog, and that a story or role play helps them get ready for a new place.

A 2-year-old is earlier in that path. The [2-year page](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html) says most children play with more than one toy at once, such as putting toy food on a toy plate, and that dress-up with a hat or a shirt is a way into pretend. A cup and one figure are enough at that age. A detailed village, with small accessories, is not.

NAEYC's [position statement on advancing equity](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/position-statements/equity) says early learning should treat diversity and inclusion as strengths, and that every child deserves to be a valued member of the group. A playset can support that when the people in it look like more than one kind of family, including the child's own. It works against it when the only "other" figure is a costume played for laughs.

Healey and Mendelsohn, in [Selecting Appropriate Toys for Young Children in the Digital Era](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30509931/) (*Pediatrics*, 2019), say children learn from toys mainly because a caregiver is in the play. The set can be wood, plastic, or a shoebox. The conversation is the part that carries the place.

## Set up one scene

Use one place per week. A market, a home, a bus, or a park is enough. Leave the rest of the pieces in a box.

1. **Start from a routine the child has.** Eating, sleeping, saying hello, or riding somewhere. The CDC's pretend examples are daily roles, not a lecture.
2. **Put out a few figures and one prop.** A bed, a cup, a bag. If a piece fits in the small-parts cylinder, it stays in the box. The cylinder is 1.25 inches across and 2.25 inches deep. The rule is the same one in <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-for-toddlers">safe toys for toddlers</Link>.
3. **Open one real source.** A children's book of ordinary life in that place, or one photo of a real street, kitchen, or school. Point at the picture and name one thing: "They are eating." Ask what the child sees. Stop after one question.
4. **Say what is shared, then one difference.** "We eat dinner. In this picture, dinner is on the floor." Then hand the figure back. You do not need a fact about a festival, a flag, or a famous building.
5. **Follow their story for a few minutes.** If they only put the figure in the bed, that is the play. Name it once: "You put her to bed." Then be quiet.

A second session can add one new detail from the same book. A new country can wait until this scene has been played more than once.

## What to say, and what to leave out

Say things you could check in the book or the photo.

- "This child is putting on shoes."
- "This family is riding a bus."
- "I don't know. Let's look at the picture again."

Leave these lines out:

- "This is how all people in that country look, eat, or dress."
- A joke about an accent, a name, or a smell.
- A lesson on poverty, war, or climate change. A preschool playset is the wrong tool for those. If a child asks, answer in one true sentence and return to the scene, or say you will look it up.
- "Let's pretend we don't speak the language," unless you actually speak it and can teach one real greeting. A made-up language is a game. It is not practice.

If the child already has family in that place, let them lead. Their grandparent's kitchen is a better source than a toy catalog. Ask what they want to add to the scene. One object from home, such as a cup you really use, beats a full set of miniatures.

Figures that look like the child belong in the same box as figures that do not. A set made only of "people from far away," with the child's own life left in a different basket, teaches that some people are the normal toys and some people are the lesson.

## How to choose a set

You may already own the right pieces. A dollhouse, a barn, a train, or a few wooden figures can become the scene. Buy a new set only when these are true:

- **The pieces survive the size check** for the youngest child who will play. Small food, tiny shoes, and peg people fail it often. Ages and piece size for the broader toy shelf are in the <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-3-year-olds">3-year-old</Link> and <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-4-year-olds">4-year-old</Link> guides.
- **The picture could be a real day.** Homes, markets, schools, and buses are useful. A single festival outfit, with no ordinary clothes beside it, is a costume.
- **More than one kind of person is in the box,** or you can add a figure you already have so the scene is not one cartoon of a culture.
- **Nothing electronic is required.** Lights and sounds take the story away from the child. The 2019 AAP toy report favors toys a caregiver and child use together.
- **You can name the material and wipe it.** Wooden figures and a fabric mat need the same care as any other wooden toy. Cleaning is in <Link href="/blog/clean-wooden-toys">how to clean wooden toys</Link>.

Search [CPSC recalls](https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls) before a second-hand set comes out. Retire a set with loose small parts, a battery door that opens by hand, or magnets that can come free.

## A short session

Keep it inside ten minutes the first time.

1. Lay out the scene before the child sits down, so you are not building and explaining at once.
2. Read two pages of the book, or look at one photo.
3. Hand over one figure and say, "Where does this child go?"
4. Play their answer. If they change the subject, follow that.
5. Put the set away while the story is still friendly. Ending on time makes the next session easier to start.

Two children can share the scene once they can take a turn. Give each child one figure. The cooperative-play guide covers what to do when both want the same piece. Feelings that spill over are in <Link href="/blog/social-emotional-learning">social-emotional toys</Link> and <Link href="/blog/emotional-regulation-activities">emotional regulation activities</Link>.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do playsets teach children about other cultures?

They can start a true, small conversation when a real book or photo sits beside the toys and you avoid saying that one figure represents everyone. Without that, the set is ordinary pretend, which is still worthwhile. Pretend at ages 3 and 4 is a CDC milestone whether or not the scene is set in another country.

### What age is a cultural playset for?

Simple pretend with a cup and a figure fits many 2-year-olds, if every piece passes the small-parts test. A scene about another place, with a book and a short conversation, fits better from about 3, when most children can join other children's play and talk about a picture. Detailed sets with small pieces fit later, often 4 and up, and only after you have checked the piece size against the child you have.

### What if I do not know the culture well?

Say "I don't know," and look at the book together. One accurate detail is better than a list you half remember. Playing the child's own family life is a complete way to use the same toys. You do not owe a tour of the world.

### What should I skip buying?

Tiny accessories, button batteries, loose magnets, and any set whose only human figure is a stereotype or a joke. Also skip buying a new country every week. One scene, played often, gives the child somewhere to return to.

### How is this different from pretend play?

The pretend-play guide uses household props for a 3-year-old: a pot, a doll, a hat, a box. This page adds one place beyond the child's own home, and the rule for talking about the people who live there. Use the household version first if pretend is new.

## What to try first

Choose one routine, such as dinner or bedtime. Put out two figures and one prop that pass the size check. Open one book or one photo. Say one true sentence, play for ten minutes, and put it away.
