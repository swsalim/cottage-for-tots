A bath toy for a baby or toddler is a simple object that can get wet: an open cup, a scoop, a sponge, or a shape that floats. The useful set is one or two of those, large enough to hold, easy to rinse, and put away dry. The adult is the safety part. The toy is the play part.

This page is choosing, using, cleaning, drying, and storing those toys, from birth to about age 3, plus a few bath-time games. The five-point check for any infant toy, including materials, edges, and small parts, is in <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-infants">safe toys for infants</Link>. Water play on a tray, outside the tub, is in <Link href="/blog/sensory-play-activities-toddlers">sensory play for toddlers</Link>. Pouring with cups as hand practice is in <Link href="/blog/fine-motor-activities-toddlers">fine motor activities for toddlers</Link>.

**Quick answer:** Stay within arm’s reach for the whole bath, including while you reach for a towel. Put out one or two open toys you can rinse and dry, such as a cup or a solid floater. Skip toys that trap water inside a small hole. Empty the tub when you are done. The American Academy of Pediatrics says on [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/Water-Safety-And-Young-Children.aspx), updated 18 May 2026, that young children can drown in as little as an inch or two of water, quickly and silently, and that an adult should stay within arm’s length and use touch supervision at bath time.

## How to choose by age and ability

Match the toy to what the child can already do in the water, and to whether they still put things in their mouth. A younger baby does not need a basket of toys to have a bath. [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/play-and-learning/baby-and-toddler-play-ideas/) says babies and toddlers love water, in the bath or in a bowl, and that a child learns more from you than from any toy.

| What you see | A fit for the bath | Leave it out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A baby you are holding, who cannot sit | The wash itself. One large toy to look at, only if your hand can stay on the baby | A pile of toys, anything with a small part, and any seat you would turn away from |
| Sitting with your hands still on them | One open cup or a soft washcloth | Toys with pieces that come off, and anything that holds a hidden puddle |
| A toddler who pours and reaches | One cup and one scoop, or one solid floater | A set of many pieces, squirting toys you cannot dry inside, and anything marked not for under 3 |
| Any age, still mouthing toys | One piece, too large for the small-parts cylinder, with a smooth edge | A 3+ toy, a broken toy, and an older sibling’s smaller bath set |

The CDC’s pages for [toddlers 1 to 2](https://www.cdc.gov/child-development/positive-parenting-tips/toddlers-1-2-years.html) and [toddlers 2 to 3](https://www.cdc.gov/child-development/positive-parenting-tips/toddlers-2-3-years.html) both say not to leave a toddler near water, including a bathtub, without someone watching. The 2-to-3 page, updated 29 January 2026, also says to check toys often for loose or broken parts.

A cup is already on the age shelves. Filling and dumping one is in <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-1-year-olds">educational toys for 1-year-olds</Link>. Water with a cup, and the same drowning limit of an inch or two, is in <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-2-year-olds">educational toys for 2-year-olds</Link>.

## What makes a useful infant bath toy

For a baby, simple wins. [Health Canada’s toy safety tips](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/toy-safety/toy-safety-tips.html) say to follow the age range on the package, to choose toys that feel sturdy, with no sharp edges, and to make sure parts are firmly attached. CPSC’s [toy tips](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Toys) say to follow the age guidance on the packaging and to match the toy to the child.

A useful infant bath toy has four plain traits:

- It is large enough for a whole hand, and too large to fit in the small-parts cylinder.
- It has few or no pieces that come off. Eyes, plugs, and suction cups get a tug before the first bath.
- You can see every surface that gets wet. An open cup, a scoop, and a solid shape pass. A hollow toy with a squirt hole does not.
- You can rinse it and stand it to dry. If the maker’s instructions say not to soak it, follow that.

One or two toys are enough. Each one has to be rinsed and dried, and your hands are also on the child. A second copy of the same cup does not add a new game.

The CPSC [toy safety FAQ](https://www.cpsc.gov/FAQ/Toy-Safety) says products intended for children under 3 must not contain small parts, or release small parts after use and abuse testing. A small part is anything that fits entirely, without squeezing and in any direction, inside the [small-parts cylinder](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/Small-Parts-for-Toys-and-Childrens-Products): 1.25 inches across and 2.25 inches deep. Health Canada uses a parent check that is different: if the toy or a removable part fits inside a toilet-paper roll, it is too small for children under 3. CPSC’s [note for parents](https://onsafety.cpsc.gov/blog/2011/12/20/small-parts-what-parents-need-to-know/) says a toilet-paper roll is wider and longer than the official tester, so a piece that does not fit in the roll can still be a small part. Use the cylinder. The rest of that check is in the infant toy guide.

## Bath toy ideas by type

These are types, not products. No brand, price, or rating belongs in this list. The product column is empty on purpose.

| Toy type | Possible play | Check before buying | Product pick |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Open cup | Pour, fill, dump, compare two amounts | One piece, no lid, too large for the cylinder | None yet |
| Simple scoop | Scoop and pour | The handle stays on, and the scoop is one piece | None yet |
| Solid or sealed floater | Push it, find it, give it a one-sentence story | No hole that lets water inside. Seams are closed | None yet |
| Washcloth or sponge | Squeeze, wipe, cover and uncover | A clean sponge with no loose layer. No soap left in it | None yet |
| Colander or wide strainer | Pour through holes you can see | One piece. Holes are part of the body, not plugs that pull out | None yet |
| One-piece boat shape | A short pretend trip across the tub | No loose rider, flag, or wheel. Same size check | None yet |

Leave hollow squirt toys, bath books with torn pages, and anything run by a button battery in the shop. Health Canada says to [throw away broken toys](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/toy-safety/safety-advice-specific-toy-types.html) and to check squeeze toys so a small squeaker cannot be pulled out.

If a wooden block ends up in the water, it is the wrong object for a soak. Wiping and drying wood is in <Link href="/blog/clean-wooden-toys">how to clean wooden toys</Link>.

## Safety essentials

Bath toys do not change the rules of the bath.

The AAP’s [drowning-prevention policy statement](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/158/1/e2026077410/207630/Prevention-of-Drowning-Policy-Statement) in *Pediatrics* says to provide constant adult presence when bathing an infant or young child, and never to leave children alone in the bathroom. It says never to leave, even for a moment, a young child alone or with another child in or near a bathtub. A supervising adult should be within arm’s length and provide touch supervision for an infant, toddler, or weak swimmer. The same statement says not to use alcohol or drugs while supervising, and that the adult’s job is supervision, not a phone.

[HealthyChildren.org’s toddler page](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/Water-Safety-And-Young-Children.aspx) puts the same rule in parent language: full attention, within arm’s length, touch supervision, at bath time and at swim time. It says most child drownings inside the home occur in bathtubs, usually during a lapse in supervision, and that children should not bathe alone until they are at least 6 years old. Another young sibling is not the supervisor. If you have to leave the room, take the child with you.

The CDC’s [drowning prevention page](https://www.cdc.gov/drowning/prevention/index.html), updated 29 September 2025, says more children ages 1 to 4 die from drowning than from any other cause, that drowning happens in seconds and is often silent, and that a responsible adult should supervise constantly when children are in or near water, including bathtubs. Adults should skip reading, the phone, and alcohol or drugs while they watch, because drowning happens quickly and quietly. The CDC’s [risk-factors page](https://www.cdc.gov/drowning/risk-factors/index.html), updated 27 January 2026, says that among infants under 1 year old, three quarters of drownings occur in bathtubs.

[NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/play-and-learning/baby-and-toddler-play-ideas/) says never to leave a young child alone in the bath or playing with water, and that a baby or young child can drown in less than 5 centimetres (2 inches) of water.

A few checks belong next to the toy, still with you in reach:

- **Bath seats and floating toys are not supervision.** [HealthyChildren.org’s infant water page](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/Infant-Water-Safety.aspx), updated 18 May 2026, says infant bath seats can tip over and children can slip out and drown in a few inches of water. The AAP policy says to avoid inflatable arm bands, neck rings, and floaties because they may deflate. CDC says not to rely on air-filled or foam toys, because those are not safety devices. A cup that floats is a toy. It is not a life jacket.
- **Heat.** The infant water page says to test bath water with a wrist or elbow, and that the hottest water at the faucet should be no more than 120 degrees Fahrenheit, to reduce scalds.
- **Age labels and small parts.** If a package says “not suitable for children under 36 months,” NHS says not to give it to a baby or toddler under 3. CPSC says toys that are a choking hazard for children under 3 must have an age label, and to keep small parts away from children younger than 3.
- **Damage.** Before the bath, look for cracks, loose parts, and peeling stickers. Health Canada says to stop using a toy that is cracked, broken, or falling apart. A piece that breaks off gets the cylinder test. If it fails, the toy is finished.
- **Recalls.** Before a second-hand toy comes home, search [CPSC recalls](https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls). NHS says second-hand toys and market-stall toys may not meet safety standards.

Empty the tub when the bath is over. The toddler page says to consider removing the drain plug when the tub is not in use, so a child cannot turn on the tap and fill it. Empty buckets too. The policy statement says to empty buckets and store them upside down.

This page does not teach rescue steps. The AAP says caregivers should learn CPR. Classes are a separate job from choosing a cup.

## How to clean, dry, and store

Rinse the toy, get the water out, and let air at every surface. Follow the maker’s instructions when you still have them. [Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/toy-safety/safety-advice-specific-toy-types.html) says to follow the manufacturer’s instructions for the toy. [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/how-to-clean-baby-toys), in a July 2023 article quoting pediatrician Kimberly Giuliano, MD, says the same, and names warm soapy water as the usual wash for toys that get wet.

A routine that stays inside those sources:

1. Rinse with clean water after the bath.
2. Wash with warm water and mild soap when the toy looks grimy or has been in a mouth, if the label allows it. Rinse the soap off.
3. Tip, shake, or squeeze out water you can see.
4. Air-dry until the toy is dry, including in any groove. Cleveland Clinic says to dry toys that get wet whenever you can, because mold is more likely where moisture stays.
5. Store dry toys in an open basket. Health Canada says to use open containers such as baskets or canvas bins, and never to let children play in airtight bins, trunks, or boxes.

Skip a sealed box of wet toys. That is leftover water with the lid on.

### Toys that trap water

Hollow bath toys with a small hole are a different object from a cup. Water gets in and does not come out. Cleveland Clinic says those holes are why bath toys can grow mold on the inside, where you cannot see it, and that mold grows where there is moisture and darkness.

A 2018 paper in *npj Biofilms and Microbiomes* by [Neu and colleagues](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41522-018-0050-9) looked inside bath toys rather than at a cleaning recipe. All 19 used toys, collected from five households in Switzerland, had dense, slimy biofilms on the inner surface. The average was about 9.5 million bacterial cells per square centimetre. About 70 percent had black areas the authors described as indicative of mold. Fungi were identified in 11 of the 19. In a smaller controlled test, three toys exposed to used bath water grew more biofilm than three exposed to clean water beforehand: about 73 million cells per square centimetre versus about 5.5 million. The authors report positive lab tests for at least one indicator organism or potential opportunistic pathogen in 80 percent of the biofilms they studied. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* grew from 61 percent of the real used toys. They also say those tests were not confirmed further, that a matching gene group is not the same as a proven pathogen, and that more research is needed before anyone can say how often squeezing that water at a child’s face leads to an eye, ear, wound, or stomach infection.

Use the paper for this limit: a toy that holds bath water inside can grow a biofilm you will not scrub out with a rinse. The authors’ practical line is that closing the hole is the straightforward way to stop that exposure. Cleveland Clinic’s buying notes point the same direction: a solid toy, a toy you can open and wash inside, or a toy with no hole for water to enter. The clinic also mentions plugging a hole with hot glue or drilling it larger. This page does not use those home fixes for children who mouth toys. Choose a shape you can already wash.

Replace a toy you cannot dry, a toy that cracks, and a toy that smells foul or is visibly covered in mold. Cleveland Clinic says a toy covered in mold, or one that smells foul, should be thrown away rather than saved. For a healthy child, the same pediatrician says small traces of this kind of mold are usually handled by a normal immune system. A child with a mold allergy, asthma symptoms, a weakened immune system, chemotherapy, or a transplant needs a stricter eye on wet toys. That is a reason to ask the child’s doctor, not a reason to invent a stronger cleaner.

Do not boil a bath toy because a blog says to. The 2018 paper mentions boiling and emptying water as advice that already circulates online. It does not test those steps. If the package names a wash, use that. If it is silent, use warm soapy water on surfaces you can reach, then dry the toy. A dishwasher is only appropriate when the maker says so.

## Play ideas

These are short games with the toys above. They are something to do while you are already in reach. They are not a class, and they do not assign a milestone.

[NAEYC’s notes on sand and water play](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/blog/importance-sand-and-water-play) describe preschool classrooms where pouring leads children to notice which container holds more, and where children guess what will sink or float. The article is about that classroom, with a teacher nearby. A bath at home is the same materials and you. It is not a promise of a math skill or a science skill.

### Pour and compare

Two open cups. Pour once from one into the other. Hand the cup over. Name “more” only if they look at the water. Stop at one spill you can wipe. The longer hand version, at a tray, is the fine-motor post.

### Find the floater

One solid floating shape and a washcloth. Cover the toy. Let them pull the cloth off. If they throw the cloth, the game was the pull. Put the cloth where you can reach it.

### Sink or float

Three large toys you can already name: a cup, a sponge, a solid floater. Set one on the water. Say “on top” or “under” only if they notice. The tray version of this game, with the choking skips, is the sensory-play post.

### Story boat

A one-piece boat, or the cup turned on its side. One sentence: the cup is going to the tap, or to their knee. NHS says to get involved yourself, because a child learns more from you than from any toy. A second sentence is optional. When they chew the boat, the story is over and the size check is the point.

### Water rhythm

Pat the water, or tap a cup on the surface, once, then hand it over. The CDC 2-to-3 tips include teaching a simple song. A song you already sing at bedtime works here. The toy does not need a speaker. Stop if the pat becomes a face full of water.

NHS also lists plastic bottles, a sponge, a colander, a funnel, and unbreakable spoons for water play. Use the bottle with the cap off and put away, so the cap is not a small part in the tub. Skip straws for this age.

## Before, during, and after

| Moment | What to do | Stop if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before | Set the towel and the one or two toys where you can reach them without turning away. Test the water with a wrist or elbow. Tug any loose part. | You would have to leave the child to fetch something |
| During | Keep a hand on a baby, and stay within arm’s reach of a toddler. Watch the child, not the phone | You need to leave, the child slips, or a piece comes off |
| After | Take the child out first. Then drain the tub, rinse the toys, and set them to dry in an open basket | A toy still holds water, smells foul, or is cracked |

If a piece came off during the bath, that toy does not go back in the basket.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do babies need bath toys?

No. The bath is the wash, with you holding them or within arm’s reach. NHS says children love water and learn more from the adult playing with them than from any toy. Add one large, open toy when you want a game, not because the bath is incomplete without one.

### How many bath toys should go in the tub?

One or two. You still have to see the child, rinse every toy, and dry every toy. A basket of squirting animals adds surfaces you cannot clean.

### Are squirting bath toys a mold risk?

They can hold water where you cannot scrub. Cleveland Clinic says a small hole lets moisture in and mold can grow inside. Neu and colleagues found biofilms inside all 19 used bath toys they opened, with fungi in 11 of them, and they say the straightforward way to avoid that inner surface is a toy with no hole. A rinse of the outside does not empty a hidden puddle. Prefer a cup or a solid shape, and throw a toy away if it smells foul or is covered in mold.

### How do you clean bath toys?

Rinse them, wash reachable surfaces with warm soapy water when the label allows, get the water out, and air-dry them fully. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions. Cleveland Clinic names that soapy wash and a full dry. Store the dry toys in an open basket, which matches Health Canada’s advice to use open containers.

### Can a bath seat or a floating toy replace an adult?

No. HealthyChildren.org says a bath seat can tip and a child can slip out into a few inches of water. The AAP says floaties can deflate and do not keep children safe. CDC says air-filled and foam toys are not safety devices. An adult stays within arm’s reach for the whole bath.

### When can a child bathe alone?

Not in the years this page covers. HealthyChildren.org says children should not bathe alone until they are at least 6 years old, and that another child is not the supervisor. From birth through age 3, an adult is within arm’s reach.

### What should I do with a cracked bath toy?

Stop using it. Health Canada says to throw away broken toys. A crack can become a small part, a sharp edge, or a new place for water to sit. If you think the toy was recalled, check the CPSC recall list before anyone else uses it.

## What to try first

Use one open cup you already own. Test the water, keep a hand on the child, and pour once. When the bath ends, the child comes out first. Then rinse the cup, tip it, and let it dry in the open before it goes back in a basket.
