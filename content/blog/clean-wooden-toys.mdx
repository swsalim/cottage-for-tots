Clean wooden toys with a damp cloth, a drop of mild soap, and warm water. Wring the cloth until it does not drip, wipe the toy, wipe off the soap, and let the wood dry completely before it goes back to your child. Do not soak the toy, and do not put it in the dishwasher.

That is the whole job for ordinary dirt. Disinfecting is a separate step, and most homes do not need it every week. <Link href="/blog/sustainable-educational-toys">Wooden toys</Link> last for years when they stay dry between cleanings.

## Cleaning, sanitizing, and disinfecting

These words get used as if they were the same job. They are not.

| Word | What it does | When it fits a wooden toy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cleaning | Lifts dirt, food, and dust | Every time the toy looks grimy or has been in a mouth |
| Sanitizing | Lowers bacteria on a surface | After the toy is already clean, if you have a reason to go further |
| Disinfecting | Kills bacteria and viruses on a surface | After a sick person with a fever or diarrhea has handled the toy |

[Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/babies-kids/baby-toys/how-to-safely-clean-and-disinfect-your-babys-toys-a1693351534/), in a December 2024 guide, asked the American Academy of Pediatrics about this. Pediatrician Danette Glassy told them that at home, cleaning is enough unless someone sick with a fever or diarrhea has touched the toys. A weekly clean is a reasonable rhythm. There is no study that says you must sanitize wooden toys on a schedule.

Vinegar belongs in the cleaning column. The same Consumer Reports piece says vinegar does not disinfect or sanitize, so it is the wrong tool when you are worried about germs after an illness.

## What to use

You already have what you need:

- Mild dish soap
- Warm water
- Two soft cloths, or a cloth and a soft towel
- White vinegar, if you want a fresher-smelling wipe
- A soft toothbrush for grooves

Skip bleach, ammonia, antibacterial wipes, and any cleaner you would not want on something a child mouths. Wood is porous. Liquid that sits in the grain is hard to rinse out, and leftover moisture is a place for more grime to collect.

## How to clean wooden toys

Check the toy before it gets wet. Set it aside if you find splinters, cracks, loose parts, or paint that is lifting. A damaged toy is a repair or a retirement, not a cleaning project.

1. Dust off loose dirt with a dry cloth.
2. Add a drop of mild dish soap to a small bowl of warm water. The water should feel barely soapy.
3. Dip a soft cloth and wring it hard. It should be damp, not dripping.
4. Wipe the whole toy, including grooves. Use the toothbrush where a cloth will not reach. Do not drop the toy into the bowl.
5. Wipe again with a second cloth dampened in plain water, so soap does not stay in the grain.
6. Blot with a dry towel.
7. Leave the toy on a rack or a clean towel, out of direct sun and away from heaters, until it is dry all the way through.

[Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/babies-kids/baby-toys/how-to-safely-clean-and-disinfect-your-babys-toys-a1693351534/) notes that drying matters as much as washing, because moisture left in wood can grow more germs. Their sources suggest letting wooden toys air-dry for at least 24 hours after a clean before children play with them again.

If the maker printed care instructions, follow those first. Some finishes hate even a damp cloth.

## Vinegar and water

A light vinegar wipe is useful for sticky fingerprints and a stale smell. It is still a cleaner, not a disinfectant.

Mix 1 part white vinegar with 10 parts water. That is the dilution wooden-toy makers use for a routine wipe. Dampen a cloth with the mix, wipe the toy, then dry it at once with a towel. The smell fades as the wood dries.

Test a hidden spot first if the toy is painted, stained, or waxed. Vinegar can dull some finishes. Keep undiluted vinegar off the wood.

## How to clean wooden blocks

A big set of blocks is slow to wipe one by one. Plain, unfinished blocks can take a quick dip. Painted, stained, or oiled blocks should stay on the wipe method above.

For plain blocks:

1. Fill a sink with warm water and a drop of mild soap.
2. Swish the blocks through the water. This is a dip, not a bath. [KidKraft’s care guide](https://www.kidkraft.com/blogs/made-for-play/how-to-clean-wooden-toys), updated in March 2025, makes the same distinction: get them wet, then get them out.
3. Lift them straight away. Do not leave them sitting in the water.
4. Rinse each block with a cloth dampened in plain water.
5. Blot, then air-dry in a single layer until every face is dry. Blocks stacked while damp will stain each other.

A soft toothbrush clears crayon and dried food from the edges. If a block has split or grown rough, sanding an unfinished block is reasonable. Sanding a painted block takes the colour with the splinter, so retire that one instead.

## Second-hand wooden toys

Washing does not make an old toy safe. Look at the age of the piece before you look at the dirt.

The [CDC’s lead-prevention guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/lead-prevention/prevention/consumer-products.html) says children should not play with recalled toys, toys made before 2009, or vintage and antique products, because older items may contain lead-based paint or higher lead levels in the material. A cloth cannot remove lead from paint. If the toy is from before 2009, or you cannot tell, do not put it in a child’s hands.

For a recent second-hand toy that passes that check:

- Look for a recall notice from the maker or from the product-safety office where you live.
- Reject anything with a musty or moldy smell, soft spots, cracks, or chipped paint.
- Wash it with the soap-and-cloth method, then dry it fully before the first play session.
- If the smell remains after it is dry, do not keep it.

## After someone has been ill

Clean the toys the sick child mouthed or handled before they go back into play. Soap and water is the first step even if you plan to disinfect afterwards. Disinfectant does not work well on a dirty surface.

Consumer Reports’ 2024 conversation with the American Academy of Pediatrics is the home rule of thumb: cleaning is enough unless the illness included a fever or diarrhea. If you want to go further, use a product whose label says it is a sanitizer or disinfectant for that surface, and that it is safe for something a child may put in their mouth. In the United States, that label carries an EPA registration number. Elsewhere, follow the directions printed for your own country. Leave the surface wet for the contact time on the label, rinse if the label says to rinse, and keep children out of the room until the toy is dry.

Do not soak wood in bleach. Do not mix bleach with vinegar, ammonia, or any other cleaner. That mix can give off a toxic gas. If the label and the wood do not agree, stay with soap and water.

## Drying and storing

Dry wood stays sound. Wet wood warps, cracks, and lifts paint.

- Air-dry on a towel or rack, with space between toys.
- Keep them out of direct sun, and away from radiators, hairdryers, and hot cars.
- Wait until the toy is cool and dry in the joints, not only dry to the touch, before it goes back in the box. A full day is a safe wait after a thorough wipe.
- Store toys in a dry cupboard. A cotton bag or an open basket beats a sealed plastic bin, which traps leftover moisture.
- Give each toy room so pieces do not scratch each other.

If an unfinished toy looks faded after many washes, a tiny amount of food-grade mineral oil or beeswax on a cloth can refresh the surface once the wood is fully dry. Wipe off the excess. Wait before a baby mouths it again. Skip oil on painted toys.

## What to skip

| Skip this | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Soaking or the dishwasher | Wood swells, glue lets go, and paint lifts |
| Bleach soaks and mixed cleaners | Harsh on wood, and bleach mixed with other cleaners can release toxic gas |
| Antibacterial wipes | The liquid stays in the grain |
| Direct heat or a long sit in the sun | The wood can crack |
| Baking soda on a painted finish | The powder can scratch and dull the colour |
| Sandpaper on painted toys | You sand off the paint with the mark |

Baking soda and water can shift a stuck stain on bare wood. Use a soft cloth, a small amount, and rinse the paste off. Leave it off anything with a finish.

## How often to clean

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Everyday dust | A dry cloth when you notice it |
| A toy that lives in a mouth | Wipe with soapy water that day. Teethers may need this daily |
| Grimy hands, food, or the floor | Soap and water when you see it |
| A normal week at home | A weekly wipe is reasonable. It is not a medical schedule |
| Fever or diarrhea in the house | Clean before the toy is shared again. Disinfect only with a labelled product |
| A toy shared with another family | Clean and dry it before it changes hands |

Handwashing before play cuts down how often the toys need a wash. A sick day is a reason to clean. A quiet week is not a reason to soak the block bin.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you put wooden toys in the dishwasher?

No. The heat and the long bath warp wood, open glue joints, and lift paint. Use a damp cloth instead.

### Does vinegar disinfect wooden toys?

No. Diluted white vinegar is a useful cleaner. Consumer Reports, citing a pediatrician from the American Academy of Pediatrics in 2024, says vinegar does not disinfect or sanitize. Use it for dirt and smells. Use soap and water, and a labelled product if you truly need to disinfect, after illness.

### How do you wash wooden toy blocks?

Wipe painted or oiled blocks with a damp soapy cloth. Give plain unfinished blocks a quick swish in warm soapy water, lift them out at once, rinse with a damp cloth, and dry them in a single layer.

### How do you clean second-hand wooden toys?

Leave recalled toys, toys made before 2009, and vintage pieces out of children’s play, because older paint and materials may contain lead. The CDC states that directly. For a newer toy in good condition, check for a recall, reject mold or chipped paint, then use the soap-and-cloth method and dry it fully.

### How long should wooden toys dry?

Blot them, then air-dry away from sun and heaters. Consumer Reports’ 2024 guidance is to allow at least 24 hours after cleaning before the toy goes back to a child.

## Conclusion

Wooden toys want a light hand. A damp cloth, a drop of soap, a rinse wipe, and a full day of drying will handle almost every mess. Vinegar freshens a sticky toy. It does not replace a disinfectant after a fever or diarrhea.

Keep soaking, the dishwasher, and bleach mixes out of the routine. And if a second-hand toy is old, recalled, or shedding paint, cleaning will not make it safe. Choose another toy.
