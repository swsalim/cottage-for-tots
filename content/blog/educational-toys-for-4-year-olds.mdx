Choose educational toys for 4 year olds by the skill they are already practicing, not by the word “educational” on the box. One rhyming book, a modest set of blocks, a large ball, a hat, and one short turn-taking game cover more of this year than a pile of new toys. A toy can invite practice. It cannot guarantee a milestone.

This page is the age-4 shelf. The year before, with larger beads, a first kick, and short pretend, is in <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-3-year-olds">educational toys for 3-year-olds</Link>. The wider buying test, from babies through school age, is in the <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-child-development">guide to choosing educational toys</Link>. Each skill below is short on purpose. The full game, the full hand-toy list, and the full board-game test live in the posts linked there. ## What most 4-year-olds are practicing

The CDC [Learn the Signs. Act Early.](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html) checklists describe what most children, 75% or more, do by a given age. They are not a shopping list or a screening test. The CDC says that on its [key points](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/key-points.html) page, and the materials are not a substitute for a validated screening tool.

By 4 years, most children pretend to be something else during play, such as a teacher, a superhero, or a dog. Most ask to go play with other children if none are around. Most comfort someone who is hurt or sad. Most say sentences of four or more words, say some words from a song, story, or nursery rhyme, and talk about at least one thing that happened during the day. Most answer a simple “what is it for?” question. Most name a few colors, tell what comes next in a well-known story, and draw a person with three or more body parts. Most catch a large ball most of the time, serve themselves food or pour water with an adult watching, unbutton some buttons, and hold a crayon or pencil between fingers and thumb rather than in a fist. Those lines are on the CDC [4-year checklist](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html), reviewed 16 May 2026.

A child who is 4 and not yet catching a ball, or not yet drawing a person, is not behind a shopping deadline. Match the toy to the child in front of you.

| Skill | What you watch them do | Look for | Leave behind |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sounds and stories | A rhyme, a four-word sentence, what happens next | A picture book you read together | A toy that says every line, loose magnets |
| Numbers | A count of real things, a few color names | A small set of blocks, a short 1-to-10 path | A worksheet toy, a 100-piece kit |
| Hands and drawing | Fingers-and-thumb hold, a person if they draw one | A short crayon, paper, a few large pieces | A grip drill, tiny beads if they still mouth toys |
| Big movement | Catch a large ball, run, climb only what is meant to be climbed | A large soft ball, a clear patch of floor | Toys that shoot, a tricycle in the street |
| Turns | Ask to play, wait, comfort a friend | A two-player game that ends quickly | A long rulebook, a game that knocks someone out at once |
| Pretend roles | Stay in a role, such as the cook or the teacher | A hat, a doll, a few blocks | A scripted set with 40 pieces |
| Why and what if | A question, a pour, a tower that falls | Blocks, a cup of water you supervise | A chemistry set, loose high-powered magnets |

NAEYC’s notes on [toys for 3- to 6-year-olds](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/topics/play/toys) say children this age talk a lot, ask questions, like to experiment, and can take turns, and that they do not like to lose. Materials they can use in more than one way are the filter for every row.

## What to look for at this age

At 3, the useful toy is often one repeated hand job or one kick. At 4, look for a toy that needs the child to say something, count something, or stay in a role. The American Academy of Pediatrics, on [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/news/Pages/Best-Toys-Go-Back-To-The-Basics.aspx), says the most educational toy is one that fosters interaction between a caregiver and a child. The same organization’s [toy-buying notes](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/pages/What-to-Look-for-in-a-Toy.aspx) say to be cautious when a label says “educational,” and that digital toys should not replace face-to-face play.

Read the age label, then look at this child. The CPSC [small-parts ban](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/Small-Parts-for-Toys-and-Childrens-Products) (16 CFR part 1501) covers products intended for children under 3. A toy graded for older children can include smaller pieces, and toys that are or contain a small part, a latex balloon, a small ball, or a marble must carry a choking-hazard warning for the ages named in the rule ([16 CFR 1500.19](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-II/subchapter-C/part-1500/section-1500.19), explained in the CPSC [small-parts FAQ](https://www.cpsc.gov/FAQ/Small-Parts)). HealthyChildren.org’s [safe-toy tips](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-home/Pages/How-to-Buy-Safe-Toys.aspx) still say to think large: parts larger than the child’s mouth. If this 4-year-old still puts toys in their mouth, stay on the <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-3-year-olds">age-3 shelf</Link>.

One rule you can say in a sentence is enough. A game that ends while they still want a turn beats a set with a long setup. NAEYC notes that sharing one toy is often possible for older preschoolers, and that losing is still hard. Prefer a short game, or a shared build, over a contest that lasts half an hour.

## Early literacy and phonological awareness

The language toy at 4 is still a book you read, plus a reason to hear that words have parts. By 4, most children say some words from a song, story, or nursery rhyme, use sentences of four or more words, and tell what comes next in a well-known story. The CDC [4-year tips](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html) say to read together, ask what is happening and what might happen next, and to speak in complete sentences.

Hearing a rhyme or a first sound is a different job from owning letters. Those short games, and the research notes behind them, are in <Link href="/blog/reading-readiness-activities-preschoolers">reading readiness activities for preschoolers</Link>. How to read the book is in <Link href="/blog/reading-aloud-language-development">reading aloud</Link>. This page does not repeat them.

**Look for:** one picture book with a rhyme or a repeated line, and a puppet or hat that gives them a line to say.

**Skip:** a toy that speaks the whole story so the child never has to, and loose magnetic letters. The CPSC [magnet page](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Magnets) says high-powered magnets are a risk from toddler age through the teen years.

## Early math and number sense

At 3, “which tower has more?” is enough. At 4, the next step is counting real objects and noticing order. The CDC 4-year tips say to count simple things, such as fingers, toys, or bananas, and to talk about colors, shapes, and sizes during the day. Most 4-year-olds name a few colors. NAEYC’s 3- to 6-year-old list includes collections children can sort by quantity, not only by color.

The clearest counting toy is a short path of squares numbered 1 to 10, where the child says each number as they move. How to choose that game, and what the studies actually measured, is in <Link href="/blog/board-games-cognitive-skills">educational board games for ages 4 to 6</Link>. Home questions with blocks, paper, and water are in <Link href="/blog/preschool-stem-activities-at-home">preschool STEM activities</Link>.

**Look for:** a modest set of blocks you can count, or a path that stops at 10. Two players is enough.

**Skip:** a talking math toy that announces the answer, and a kit of 100 tiny pieces. If a younger child is on the floor, the pieces still follow the under-3 size rule.

## Fine motor and pre-writing

Keep this row short. By 4, most children hold a crayon or pencil between fingers and thumb, not in a fist, draw a person with three or more body parts, and unbutton some buttons. What those marks mean, and what not to demand, is in <Link href="/blog/scribbles-childhood-education">what scribbles mean</Link>. The toy types, from lacing to puzzles, are in <Link href="/blog/fine-motor-toys-preschoolers">fine motor toys for preschoolers</Link>. How hands compare with running and climbing is in <Link href="/blog/gross-fine-motor-skills">gross versus fine motor skills</Link>.

**Look for:** a short crayon, a large sheet of paper, and a puzzle with pieces you can see across the room. NAEYC’s preschool list starts around 12 to 20 pieces, once the child is past a toddler knob puzzle and is not mouthing the pieces. When to leave the knob board is in <Link href="/blog/wooden-puzzles-for-toddlers">wooden puzzles for toddlers</Link>.

**Skip:** a pencil-grip trainer used as a drill, and beads small enough to inhale if they still mouth toys. Crayons are exempt from the small-parts ban because they cannot be made so they never break. That exemption is not a promise a bitten crayon is safe. Stay beside a child who chews them.

## Gross motor

By 4, most children catch a large ball most of the time. That is the movement toy for this year: a ball big enough to catch, in a clear space. The World Health Organization’s 2019 guidelines, in the [Pan American Health Organization summary](https://www.paho.org/en/news/24-4-2019-grow-healthy-children-need-sit-less-and-play-more), say children ages 3 to 4 should spend at least 180 minutes a day in physical activity of any intensity, of which at least 60 minutes is moderate to vigorous, spread through the day. Walking, dancing, and a game of catch all count. One ball does not have to fill the day.

NAEYC includes tricycles, wagons, and balls for throwing and catching in the 3- to 6-year-old list. HealthyChildren.org’s [safety notes for ages 2 to 4](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Safety-for-Your-Child-2-to-4-Years.aspx) say not to let a child ride a tricycle in the street or the driveway, and to keep a soft surface under play equipment: mats or loose fill at least 9 inches deep, extending at least 6 feet out. The [4-year checkup list](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/Your-Childs-Checkups/Pages/your-checkup-checklist-4-years-old.aspx) says a 4-year-old is not ready to cross the street alone and still needs an adult during outside play and around water.

**Look for:** a soft ball much larger than the child’s mouth, a clear floor, and a tricycle only where cars cannot reach it.

**Skip:** toys that shoot objects. HealthyChildren.org’s safe-toy tips say they can injure eyes or cause choking. Balloons stay out. The required latex-balloon warning in 16 CFR 1500.19 says children under 8 can choke or suffocate on uninflated or broken balloons. Toddler push-toy lists are in <Link href="/blog/gross-motor-toys-toddlers">gross motor toys for toddlers</Link>, if the child is still on that earlier shelf.

## Social play and turn-taking

Most 4-year-olds ask to play with other children and can comfort a friend who is sad. The CDC tips say to help them use words, such as “Can I have a turn?”, and to let them play with other children at a park, a library, or a play group. They also say to give the child time to solve a problem with a friend, such as who gets to be which superhero, while you stay nearby.

NAEYC says children this age can take turns and often do not like to lose. A shared goal is an easier first game than a winner and a loser. The toy types for that, from a joint build to a parachute, are in <Link href="/blog/cooperative-play-toys-sharing">cooperative play toys</Link>. A number path with one rule is the board-game post, not this one.

**Look for:** a two-player game you can finish in a few minutes, or blocks you build together. Two children is enough when joining is still new.

**Skip:** a rule so long that you are the one playing, and any game that removes a player in the first minute.

## Imaginative play

The new line at 4 is the role. Most children pretend to be something else, not only push a car or pat a doll. The CDC tips say to offer dress-up clothes, pots and pans, or blocks, and to join in, including eating the pretend food they cook. HealthyChildren.org describes this symbolic play as a way children use words and stories to imitate real events and feelings, and it says to offer a wide range of props, including dolls, vehicles, and blocks, rather than a single script.

Household setups with a pot, a doll, and a hat are already written for age 3 in <Link href="/blog/pretend-play-social-skills">pretend play ideas</Link>. They still work. At 4, let the role last longer if the child stays in it. Naming the feeling is in <Link href="/blog/social-emotional-learning">social-emotional toys</Link>.

**Look for:** one hat or scarf, one doll or soft animal, a few blocks that can be the house. HealthyChildren.org says to remove loose ribbons or strings from stuffed toys, and to check that parts and seams stay on.

**Skip:** a 40-piece pretend set and a toy that plays the whole scene so the child only watches.

## Early science and curiosity

Most of the science at 4 is a question you answer, including “I don’t know.” The CDC tips say to take time for “why” questions, and to look up an answer together in a book when you do not know it. NAEYC says preschoolers like to experiment. A tower that falls, a cup of water they pour while you watch, or “what happens if we add one more block?” is the activity. Those questions are written out in <Link href="/blog/preschool-stem-activities-at-home">preschool STEM activities</Link>.

**Look for:** blocks, a cup, and one question. The pour is already on the CDC movement list: most 4-year-olds serve food or pour water with an adult watching.

**Skip:** hobby kits and chemistry sets. HealthyChildren.org says to avoid them for any child younger than 12, because they can cause fires or contain dangerous chemicals. Skip loose high-powered magnets, sold as science toys or not. Water beads stay out of a room shared with a child 3 or younger. The [CPSC water-bead alert](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/SafetyAlert_5101_WaterBeads_082024.pdf) says to remove them from places with children that age.

## Safety

Safety at 4 is the label plus the child, not a new set of hazards invented for this birthday.

HealthyChildren.org’s safe-toy tips say to read the warning label, show the child how the toy is used, choose sturdy plastic rather than thin plastic that cracks into sharp pieces, and look for a “nontoxic” label. Stuffed toys should be well made and washable, without loose strings or bean-like pellets. Electric toys should be UL approved. The same page says to be careful with button batteries or lithium coin batteries and high-powered magnets, because a loose one can be swallowed or placed in a nose or ear. Recalled toys should be removed. The CPSC lists recalls at [cpsc.gov](https://www.cpsc.gov/).

Age grades tell you whether the maker intended the toy for this age, including choking hazards. They do not mean this child is done mouthing toys, and they do not replace supervision. The 4-year checkup list says to watch outside play and water, and that a 4-year-old does not cross the street alone.

## How to play together

Put the toy between you and play once. The CDC 4-year tips are specific: join the pretend meal, count the blocks or the bananas, answer the why question, and help with the words for a turn. If two children argue over roles, stay close and let them try before you solve it.

One comment is enough. “You put the red block on the blue one” names what they did. A quiz of ten questions ends the game. Then stop talking and let them change the story.

Screens are a weak stand-in for that. The CDC 4-year tips say to limit screen time to no more than 1 hour a day of a children’s program with an adult present, and not to put a screen in the bedroom. HealthyChildren.org says digital toys and apps should not replace face-to-face play, including the gestures and back-and-forth that a tablet does not offer.

Put out one or two toys. A floor covered in every new thing makes the choice harder. There is no researched correct number of toys for a 4-year-old. Buy when a skill you see this month has no material, or when the pieces they have are cracked, too small for this child, or too frustrating to repeat. A book, a ball, a box of blocks, a crayon, and a hat cover the table.

## Frequently asked questions

### What educational toys does a 4-year-old need?

A few they will use: a book you read together, something to count or build, a large ball, a crayon and paper, and one prop for a role or a short turn. Match the toy to the skill you see this month. The CDC list is a reference for what most children do, not a cart.

### Are electronic learning toys a good buy at age 4?

Sometimes, when the child still has to do something: match, turn a page, or press once. HealthyChildren.org says the most educational toy fosters interaction, and that digital toys should not replace face-to-face play. A toy that speaks every word and plays the whole game leaves them less to say. Prefer the book, the blocks, or the hat when the goal is language or pretend play.

### What if my 4-year-old still mouths toys?

Use the under-3 size rule on the <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-3-year-olds">age-3 page</Link>. Large pieces only. No water beads, no tiny beads, no loose magnets, no balloons, no button batteries. A 4+ label does not cancel mouthing.

### Do I need a separate toy for each skill?

No. Blocks can be a tower, a count, a house, and a reason to ask what happens if you add one more. A hat can be pretend play and a sentence. The table is a way to notice a gap, not a requirement to own seven categories.

### How is this different from the age-3 page and the single-skill posts?

The age-2 page, <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-2-year-olds">educational toys for 2-year-olds</Link>, is about 24 to 36 months, while the under-3 piece-size rule is still in force. The age-3 page is hands, movement, words, short pretend, and building for 3-year-olds, including larger beads and piece size for children who still mouth toys. This page is age 4: rhymes and what happens next, counting real objects, a fingers-and-thumb hold, catching a large ball, a turn with another child, a role they stay in, and a why question. Fine-motor toys, reading-readiness games, STEM activities, cooperative games, and the 1-to-10 board-game path each have their own post. This one only says which of those to open.

## What to try first

Watch one play session. Write down the skill they repeat: a rhyme, a count, a catch, a role, or a “why.” Pull out one toy for that skill. Leave the other rows for another week.

Then read the matching post linked above if you would rather not buy, and the <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-child-development">educational toys guide</Link> if you want the safety check in full. If they are closer to 3 than to 4, or they still mouth toys, use the <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-3-year-olds">age-3 page</Link> instead.
