A social-emotional toy is useful when it gives a child one feeling to name, one person or animal to care for, or one turn to wait for. You do not need a special “SEL” set. A doll, a puppet, dress-up clothes, or a short game you already own can do the job if you stay nearby and use a few words.

These toys are practice. They are not a lesson plan, and they are not treatment for anxiety, aggression, or a developmental condition.

## What social-emotional learning means in play

[CASEL](https://casel.org/what-is-sel/) describes social-emotional learning as the process of managing emotions, working toward goals, showing empathy, keeping supportive relationships, and making caring decisions. The same framework names five areas: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills, and responsible decision-making.

Those words are written for schools and for a whole childhood, not for a product label. At home they look smaller:

| CASEL area | What it can look like at age 2 to 5 | A toy that fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-awareness | Pointing to a face and hearing “sad” or “happy” | A mirror, a feelings book, a doll with a clear expression |
| Self-management | Stopping when the music stops, or hugging a toy while upset | A song, a stuffed animal, a simple stop-and-go game |
| Social awareness | Noticing that someone else is crying | A doll or figure you can “check on” |
| Relationship skills | A turn, a greeting, or playing the customer and the cook | Dress-up, a puppet, a short cooperative game |
| Responsible decision-making | Choosing the gentle option you just showed | Any toy with a real choice, such as sharing one ball |

A toy can sit in more than one row. The test is whether your child can do something with it, not whether the box prints the word empathy.

## Match the toy to the age

The CDC’s [Learn the Signs. Act Early.](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html) checklists describe what 75% or more of children do by a given age. They are not a screening test. If something on the list is missing and it worries you, talk with your child’s doctor.

[NAEYC](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/topics/play/toys) groups pretend toys the same way across these ages: dolls, puppets, dress-up clothes, child-sized furniture, and play food. What changes is how long the play lasts and whether another child can join.

| Age | Social milestone most children show | Play that matches it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 years | Notices when someone is hurt or upset. Looks at your face to see how to react in a new situation. | A soft doll, a hat and a shirt for dress-up, and blocks you take turns stacking. Children this age often play beside a friend more than with them. |
| 3 years | Calms down within about 10 minutes after you leave, such as at drop-off. Notices other children and joins them. | A puppet, a play kitchen, or one short game. Joining is new, so keep the group small. |
| 4 years | Pretends to be someone else, such as a teacher or a dog. Comforts a friend who is hurt or sad. Asks to play with another child. | Dress-up with a role, figures, and a game where friends are not trying to beat each other. Losing still ends a lot of preschool play. |

Sources for the milestone column: CDC checklists for [2 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html), [3 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html), and [4 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html), reviewed 16 May 2026.

## Dolls and soft animals

A doll is the clearest social-emotional toy for this age, because the child can feed it, cover it, and decide how it feels.

A 2020 study in *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* measured brain activity in 33 children ages 4 to 8 while they played. During solo play, a region linked with social processing (the posterior superior temporal sulcus) was more active with dolls than with a creative tablet game. Playing with another person activated that region for both kinds of play. A 2022 follow-up in *Developmental Science*, with the same age group, found that children talked more about other people’s feelings, wants, and thoughts during doll play than during tablet play, especially when they were alone.

Read those results narrowly. The children were 4 to 8, not toddlers. The dolls were Barbie sets. Mattel paid for the equipment and some staff and joined early design talks. The paper says Mattel did not have the final say on the design and did not collect, analyze, or write up the results. The finding is about doll play as a kind of pretend play. It is not proof that one brand builds empathy, and it is not a reason to hand a 2-year-old a tablet “feelings” app.

At age 2, skip the brain claim and use the simpler CDC observation: many children this age already pause when someone is upset. A soft doll with embroidered eyes, no button pieces, gives them someone to pat. Offer one feeling word, then stop. “The baby is sad.” If they keep patting, you do not need a second sentence.

## Puppets and dress-up

NAEYC lists puppets and dress-up clothes as pretend toys from the toddler years into preschool. CDC’s own 2-year activity ideas include grown-up clothes such as shoes, hats, and shirts, so the child can start pretending. By 4, most children pretend to be someone else during play.

Keep the costume small. One hat is a role. A full bin of clothes is a pile to dump.

A puppet helps when your child will not answer “How do you feel?” Ask the puppet instead, or let the puppet say one thing that happened today. CDC’s 4-year language list includes talking about at least one thing from the day. The puppet can be the one who says it.

Stay out of the way once the story starts. If you quiz them on the moral, the play usually stops.

## Games that ask children to wait

Preschoolers can take turns, and they often hate to lose. NAEYC says that directly about ages 3 to 6. A race-to-the-end game is a hard first lesson in feelings. A game with one shared goal is easier: move one piece together, add one block each, or pass one ball.

Short phrases are enough: “my turn,” “your turn.” End the game while it is still friendly. Waiting practice also shows up without a board. CDC’s 4-year tips include freeze dance: music on, music off, bodies still. That is self-management with no pieces to lose.

## Feelings books and a mirror

A book with clear faces, or a mirror at child height, is enough for naming. Sit together. Point to one face. Say the word. Make the same face yourself.

Two or three words are plenty at first: happy, sad, mad. Add “surprised” or “scared” when those show up in real life, not because a poster has twelve faces.

Emotion cards and egg-shaped feeling toys are popular. Use them only when every piece is large. For children under 3, a [small part](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/Small-Parts-for-Toys-and-Childrens-Products) is anything that fits entirely, without squeezing and in any orientation, inside the small-parts cylinder (1.25 inches across and 2.25 inches deep). Household cards and game tokens are not tested the way toys are. If a piece would fit in that cylinder, do not give it to a child who still mouths objects.

## Seven ways to use the toys this week

You can do these with materials you already have. One a day is enough.

1. **Pat the doll.** When someone in a book or in the room is upset, hand your child the doll and say “sad” once. CDC’s 4-year example is similar: “He looks sad. Let’s bring him a teddy.”
2. **One hat, one job.** A hat, a spoon, and a bowl. They are the cook. You are the customer. Trade. This is the dress-up play CDC suggests from age 2, kept to a single role.
3. **Puppet reports the day.** After preschool or the park, the puppet says one thing that happened. Your child can correct the puppet. That is easier than a direct question.
4. **Mirror pair.** Both of you look in the mirror and make one face. Name it. Stop after one or two faces.
5. **Three-block turns.** CDC suggests taking turns building a tower and knocking it down around age 2. One block each, then the crash, which is the fun part.
6. **Freeze.** Play music and stop it. Still bodies. Start again. Use this when waiting in a game is still too abstract.
7. **Helper, not audience.** Let them carry napkins or plastic cups to the table. CDC lists that help at age 2. Thank them for the job. Helping is a relationship skill with a real result. Related hands-on jobs for 2-year-olds are in <Link href="/blog/montessori-activities-2-year-olds">Montessori-inspired activities</Link>.

If the play turns into art, stories, or building with no feelings script, let it. Open-ended play still carries the social part. More on that is in <Link href="/blog/creative-play-grow">creative play</Link> and the <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-child-development">guide to choosing educational toys</Link>.

## What to skip

Skip a toy that only lectures. If the child presses a button and a voice names “kindness,” they have not practiced anything.

Skip solo feeling apps as the main tool. CDC’s activity notes limit young children’s screen time and say children learn by talking, playing, and being with people. The doll studies above also found less of the measured social-brain activity during solo tablet play than during solo doll play. A screen can sit beside you for a shared story. It is a weak substitute for the doll.

Skip competitive games as the first feelings toy if losing still causes a meltdown. Come back to them when your child can lose one round and stay in the room. Until then, use a shared tower, freeze dance, or the games in the cooperative-play guide.

Skip sets with tiny faces, beads, or magnets for any child under 3, and for older children who still put things in their mouth.

## If play is hard right now

Some children want the doll and no other children. Solo doll play still gave 4- to 8-year-olds a chance to talk about someone else’s feelings in the 2022 study. Parallel play at age 2 is expected. CDC notes that 2-year-olds play next to each other and still need an adult to show sharing.

Some children throw the toy when they are upset. Take the thrown object away if it can hurt someone, and offer the same calming options CDC suggests at age 3: a breath, a hug for a favorite toy, or a quiet spot. Return the game later. A lecture in the middle of the throw does not stick.

Toys do not diagnose or treat. If your child stays very upset most days, has lost skills, or you are worried, tell your child’s doctor and ask about developmental screening. The CDC checklists say the same thing: you know your child best, and waiting is the wrong plan when you are concerned.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are social-emotional toys?

They are ordinary toys that make a feeling, a role, or a turn obvious. Dolls, puppets, dress-up clothes, feelings books, and short cooperative games qualify. A toy is not social-emotional because the package says so. It qualifies when a child can practice one of those three things with it.

### Do I need to buy a social-emotional learning set?

No. A doll or stuffed animal, a hat, a mirror, and blocks cover the activities on this page. Buy a puppet or a simple cooperative game when your child already pretends and wants another person in the story.

### What if my child only plays alone?

Alone is still useful, especially with a doll. The Cardiff studies found solo doll play lined up with social thinking in 4- to 8-year-olds. At age 2, playing beside another child, rather than with them, is common. Invite one friend for a short tower game when your child looks interested. Do not force a group.

### Is this the same as Serious Play?

No. Serious Play, including LEGO Serious Play, is a facilitated workshop method that uses bricks with groups, usually adults. It is not a preschool toy category. This page is about dolls, puppets, dress-up, and short games for toddlers and preschoolers.

### Can these toys fix hitting, anxiety, or tantrums?

They can be a place to practice a calmer action you have already shown, such as patting a doll or stopping when the music stops. They do not treat anxiety or behavior conditions. If those problems are frequent, or you are worried about development, talk with your child’s doctor.

## What to try first

Pick one toy your child already likes. Use it once today with a single phrase: “sad,” “your turn,” or “you are the cook.” Put that toy where they can reach it tomorrow, and leave the rest of the bin put away.
