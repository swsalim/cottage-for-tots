At ages 4 to 6, the useful board game is short, has one rule, and ends while the child still wants a turn. A path of squares numbered 1 to 10, where they say each number as they move, is the format with the clearest evidence. This page is how to choose that kind of game. It is not a ranking of boxes we tested.

Shared-goal games, where nobody has to lose, are in <Link href="/blog/cooperative-play-toys-sharing">cooperative play toys</Link>. Holding one rule for 10 to 20 minutes is covered in <Link href="/blog/toys-improve-focus-concentration">toys for concentration</Link>.

## What a counting path actually changed

A [2008 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18366429/) of preschoolers from low-income families (mean age 5.4 years) found that about one hour with a linear number board game improved four skills: comparing which number is larger, placing numbers on a line, counting, and naming written numerals. The gains were still there 9 weeks later. Classmates who played the same game with colors instead of numbers did not improve on those measures.

A [2009 follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18801120/) used four 15-minute sessions. The number game improved number estimation. The color game did not. In that study, the short run of number play closed a gap the researchers had measured between children from lower-income and higher-income families. That is one set of studies, on one simple path, not a promise that every boxed game raises a score.

The home version is the same idea. Draw ten squares in a line. Number them 1 to 10. Use a move of 1 or 2 at first. If the child is on 4 and moves 2, they say “5, 6” as they pass those squares. About 15 minutes is a full session. Four short sessions matter more than one long night.

By age 5, most children can count to 10 and name some numbers from 1 to 5 when you point to them, on the [CDC 5-year checklist](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html) (reviewed 16 May 2026). Those are milestones for about 75 percent of children, not a test a game has to pass. The checklist notes are on the [CDC key points page](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/key-points.html). A child who is still learning 1 to 5 should stay on a path that short.

## What to look for

Match the game to the skill you want this month. Two players is enough. A table of four means a long wait, and the CDC 5-year list puts ordinary attention around 5 to 10 minutes, not counting screen time.

| Number path, 1 to 10 | Age 4 to 5 | Counting, which number is bigger | 10 to 15 minutes | 2 | Squares in a line. They say each number as they move. |

### A number path

This is the first buy, or the first game you draw. Ten squares. Numbers in order. The child names the squares they move through, not only the square they land on.

A color path is a fine rainy-day game. It is not the same practice. The studies above used the numbers.

### Memory and one-rule match

Six to eight pairs is plenty. Turn two cards. If they match, they stay up. If they do not, turn them back. Stop when the child is still willing.

A one-rule game is “find the red ones” or “find the circles.” Add a second rule only after the first is easy. Sound and letter games that do this without a board are in <Link href="/blog/reading-readiness-activities-preschoolers">reading readiness activities</Link>.

### A race with a choice

Around ages 5 to 6, a short race can include one decision: which way to move, or which card to play. If the spinner decides every turn, the child is waiting, not choosing.

A 2023 trial of board and card games followed 68 children ages 7 to 12, which is older than this shelf. Both groups improved on flexible thinking and on holding back an impulse, and games with less luck still used those skills. The details, and why that is not a promise, are in <Link href="/blog/toys-improve-focus-concentration">toys for concentration</Link>. For ages 4 to 6, keep the choice small and the round short.

### A shared goal

Some children this age hate to lose. A game with one shared piece, or one problem you solve together, still practices turns. How to set that up is in <Link href="/blog/cooperative-play-toys-sharing">cooperative play toys</Link>. Feelings when a game ends badly are in <Link href="/blog/social-emotional-learning">social-emotional toys</Link>.

## What to skip

- A long strategy game built for older players. If you cannot teach the first turn in one sentence, it can wait.
- A box that plays itself: lights, a voice that announces the winner, or a button the child only presses.
- Loose magnets. The CPSC [magnet page](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Magnets) describes the injury when two or more are swallowed.
- Tiny tokens, marbles, and dice small enough to swallow if a child under 3 is in the room. The shop check is in <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-for-toddlers">how to choose safe toys for toddlers</Link>.
- A second and third game of the same type while the first one is still unfinished.

Wood is easier to wipe. Cardboard is fine when the cards are large and the corners are not sharp. The material is not the skill.

## How to play it once

Put the other toys away. Explain one rule. Take the first turn yourself, slowly, and say the numbers out loud if it is the path. Then hand it over.

Do not move their piece to the “right” square. A wrong square they notice is the lesson. If they flip the board, the session is over. The game was too long, or losing was too big a deal for today. Try the shared-goal version next time.

Store the pieces in the box. After a drop, look for a crack or a token that has become small enough for a younger sibling to mouth.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a good first board game for a 4-year-old?

A path numbered 1 to 10, played with one other person, for about 15 minutes. They say each number as they move. You can draw it on paper before you buy a box.

### Do board games make children smarter?

A linear number game, for about an hour in total, improved specific counting and number skills in the preschool studies above. A color version of the same game did not. That is not a claim about IQ, and it does not transfer to every game on a shelf.

### How long should the game last?

About 10 to 15 minutes at ages 4 to 6. The concentration guide uses 10 to 20 minutes, several times a week, as the shape that showed up in executive-function studies. Stop while they would still play again tomorrow.

### What if my child hates to lose?

End that round. Switch to a game with one shared goal, or go back to the number path and cheer the counting instead of the winner. One calm loss is useful. A flipped board is a sign to stop.

### Can a younger sibling play too?

They can roll a large die or move a big shared piece while you sit there. They should not have the small tokens. If they still mouth toys, use the under-3 size rule even if the box says 4+.

## What to try first

Draw ten squares tonight. Number them. Play until someone reaches 10, with the numbers said out loud. If that session stays friendly, you already have the game the studies used. Buy a box later if you want sturdier cards, not because the paper path failed.
