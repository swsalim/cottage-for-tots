A safe toy for a toddler is one a child about 12 to 36 months old can mouth, drop, and carry without a piece, a battery, a magnet, or a cord becoming the hazard. The age on the box is one check. The child in front of you, and any younger child on the same floor, is the other.

This page is that shop checklist. Choosing toys by skill for this age is in <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-1-year-olds">educational toys for 1-year-olds</Link> and <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-2-year-olds">educational toys for 2-year-olds</Link>. The first year, including edges, crib toys, and a play mat, is in <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-infants">safe toys for infants</Link> and <Link href="/blog/baby-play-mat">how to choose a baby play mat</Link>. Cleaning a wooden toy you already own is in <Link href="/blog/clean-wooden-toys">how to clean wooden toys</Link>.

**Quick answer:** For a child under 3, or any child who still mouths toys, every separate piece has to stay out of the small-parts cylinder: 1.25 inches across and 2.25 inches deep. Skip button batteries with a door you can open by hand, loose high-powered magnets, added strings, uninflated balloons, and toys made before 2009. Read the age label, search the recall list, and stay nearby. The American Academy of Pediatrics says babies and toddlers are at the highest choking risk because they put things in their mouths. That is on [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/injuries-emergencies/Pages/Choking-Prevention.aspx), updated 27 February 2026.

## A toddler toy safety checklist

Use this before a toy comes home, and again when it has been dropped, washed, or passed down. The sections under the table are the sources for each row.

| Check | Keep it | Set it aside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small parts | The whole toy, and anything that can come off, stays out of the cylinder | It fits in the cylinder, or a knob, eye, or wheel comes off in your hand |
| Balls and marbles | A soft ball much larger than a 1.75-inch hole | A ball that passes through that hole, or any marble |
| Balloons | No latex balloons in the house with children under 8 | An uninflated or broken balloon, including pieces |
| Button battery | No coin cell, or a door that needs a screwdriver, coin, or other tool | A door that opens with a fingernail, or a loose battery |
| Magnets | No loose or high-powered magnets | Magnetic balls or cubes, or a magnet that comes off a toy |
| Cords and strings | No ribbon you tied on. A pull cord has no beads that can make a loop | A string long enough to wrap a neck, or a toy hung where a child sleeps |
| Ride-on | The age label fits. A real helmet for a scooter or other riding toy | A toy helmet used as the safety helmet |
| Climbing | A low, stable frame, with you close enough to reach them | A dresser, a shelf, or a TV used as the climber |
| Paint and plastic | An intact finish on a toy made for children | Chipped old paint, a toy made before 2009, or a vintage toy |
| Age label | The label matches this child, including whether they still mouth toys | A choking-hazard warning on a toy that will share the floor with a child under 3 |
| Recalls | The name is not on the CPSC recall list | A recalled toy, or a toy whose maker you cannot name |

## Choking hazards and the small-parts test

Toys intended for children under 3 that present a choking hazard because of small parts are banned. A small part is anything that fits entirely, without squeezing and in any direction, inside the [small-parts cylinder](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/Small-Parts-for-Toys-and-Childrens-Products): 1.25 inches across and 2.25 inches deep. The test is [16 CFR 1501.4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-II/subchapter-C/part-1501/section-1501.4). It covers the whole toy, a separate piece, and a piece that breaks off in use-and-abuse testing.

CPSC’s [2011 note for parents](https://onsafety.cpsc.gov/blog/2011/12/20/small-parts-what-parents-need-to-know/) says a toilet-paper tube is wider and longer than the official cylinder. A piece that does not fit in a tube can still be a small part. Use the dimensions, or a tester made to them.

A ball intended for children under 3 is banned if it passes, under its own weight, entirely through a circular hole 1.75 inches (44.4 mm) across. That rule is [16 CFR 1500.18(a)(17)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-II/subchapter-C/part-1500/section-1500.18). The same hole is how [16 CFR 1500.19](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-II/subchapter-C/part-1500/section-1500.19) defines a small ball. Use a soft ball you can see is much bigger than that hole. Marbles stay out.

The AAP choking page says balloons, balls, marbles, pieces of toys, and small round foods cause the most choking deaths. It also says balloons pose a choking risk through age 8. CPSC’s [toy tips](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Toys) say to keep small balls and toys with small parts away from children younger than 3, and deflated balloons away from children younger than 8. Any latex balloon has to carry the caution in 16 CFR 1500.19.

The AAP’s [toy-buying tips](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-home/Pages/How-to-Buy-Safe-Toys.aspx), updated 9 November 2026, say to make toys and parts larger than the child’s mouth, including toys that can be squeezed to fit entirely into the mouth. Stuffed toys should have parts on tight and seams that hold. Take off loose ribbons. Leave out toys filled with small bean-like pellets. Thin plastic that can break into sharp pieces fails the same pass.

Books, crayons, chalk, pencils, and similar writing materials are exempt from the small-parts ban, because they cannot be made so they never break into bits. The exemption is in 16 CFR 1500.19 and in the small-parts rule. It is not a promise that a bitten crayon is safe. Stay beside a child who chews them. What those marks mean is in <Link href="/blog/scribbles-childhood-education">what scribbles mean</Link>.

Household objects are not tested like toys. If a bead, coin, or lid would fit in the cylinder, it does not go on the floor. [Safe Kids Worldwide](https://www.safekids.org/tip/choking-and-strangulation-prevention-tips) says to get on the floor at the child’s eye level and to keep buttons, beads, jewelry, marbles, and coins out of reach. Water beads are a separate ingestion hazard. The fuller note is in <Link href="/blog/squishy-toys">squishy toys for kids</Link>. Puzzle knobs get the same cylinder test in <Link href="/blog/wooden-puzzles-for-toddlers">wooden puzzles for toddlers</Link>.

If a child cannot breathe, is gasping, cannot cry, turns bluish, grabs at the throat, or becomes limp, that is an emergency. The AAP lists those signs and says to take a certified CPR and first aid course. This page does not teach the rescue steps.

## Button batteries

A button cell or coin battery can burn through a child’s throat or esophagus in as little as two hours if it is swallowed. CPSC says that on its [button-battery page](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Button-Cell-Coin-Battery-Information-Center), and it says these batteries are associated with thousands of emergency department visits every year. The AAP toy guide says a loose button battery or lithium coin battery can also be pushed into a nose or ear.

Toys with button cell or coin batteries are required to have a secure closure that needs a screwdriver, coin, or other tool to open. Check the toys already in the house. If the door opens with a fingernail, or the compartment is cracked, that toy is finished. CPSC says to repair, replace, or dispose of a damaged compartment, because children have been injured by loose batteries. The same page says children should not play with or touch the batteries themselves.

The toy standard, ASTM F963 as incorporated in [16 CFR part 1250](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Toy-Safety), requires battery toys to keep a child from reaching the battery during play. Remotes and other products that are not toys need a screw closure too, if a child can get to them. When you take a used cell out, CPSC says to cover the terminals with non-conductive tape, such as electrical tape, and take it to a battery-collection site.

If you think a child swallowed a button battery or put one in the nose or ear, go to the emergency department. Call the National Battery Ingestion Hotline at 800-498-8666 or Poison Help at 800-222-1222 for treatment information. CPSC relays the National Capital Poison Center’s honey advice for the trip: for a child 12 months or older who swallowed a button battery in the past 12 hours, 10 mL of honey every 10 minutes, and no more than six doses. Do not delay the emergency room to find honey. That honey advice is for a swallowed battery. It is not a home treatment, and it is not for a child younger than 12 months.

CPSC’s August 2024 [proposed rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/08/13/2024-17472/safety-standard-for-toys-requirements-for-toys-containing-button-cell-or-coin-cell-batteries) on toys with button batteries describes a 2006 study in the *Journal of Pediatric Psychology* by Morrongiello, Corbett, McCourt, and Johnston. In that summary, children ages 2 to 5 were typically out of a parent’s view for about 20 percent of awake time at home, and out of both sight and hearing for about 4 percent. The door has to hold during those minutes. The document is a proposal. The closure rule above is the requirement CPSC already states for toys.

## Magnets

When two or more high-powered magnets are swallowed, they can attract each other through body tissue. CPSC’s [magnet page](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Magnets) says that attraction has led to surgery, including repair of the stomach and intestines, and that these injuries are serious and sometimes fatal. The page says the magnets are up to eight times stronger than magnets used in toys. Symptoms to watch for are abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. On an X-ray, several pieces can look like one object. If you think magnets were swallowed, seek medical care immediately.

[Safe Kids Worldwide](https://www.safekids.org/tip/choking-and-strangulation-prevention-tips) says to keep small magnets away from children, including magnets in construction sets, children’s toys, refrigerator magnets, and rare-earth desk sets, and to check play areas for a magnet that came loose.

In the mandatory toy standard, a loose magnet or magnetic component that fits in the small-parts cylinder has to have a flux index under 50. CPSC explains that in its [toy-safety business guidance](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Toy-Safety), section 4.38. An educational toy intended for children 8 and older may contain stronger magnets if it is labeled. That label is a reason to keep the set away from a toddler. The same page points to [16 CFR part 1262](https://www.cpsc.gov/FAQ/Magnets-FAQs) for certain magnet products that are not toys, such as jewelry and stress-relief sets. Those products are not a toddler toy. A wooden puzzle does not need a magnet. That pass is also in the puzzle guide.

## Cords and strings

Cords wrap a neck. The AAP says to take loose ribbons or strings off stuffed toys to avoid strangulation. Safe Kids says not to tie a string or ribbon to a pacifier or a toy, and to keep cords and strings out of reach. CPSC’s toy standard sets length and performance requirements for accessible cords, straps, and elastics on toys intended for children under 18 months in some cases and under 36 months in others. Pull toys are one of the examples CPSC names in section 4.14 of the [business guidance](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Toy-Safety).

For a parent, the checks are plain. Do not add a ribbon so a toy is easier to find. Do not hang a toy by a cord where a child sleeps. CPSC’s public summary of the cord rule does not give one length that covers every toy. A pull cord with beads or other pieces that can tangle into a loop is still a reason to leave that toy out. If you cannot tell whether a cord would pass the standard, leave it for later. Hanging crib toys are an infant rule: they come down when a baby pushes up on the hands and knees, or at 5 months, whichever comes first. That is in the infant guide and the play-mat guide.

Clothing drawstrings, necklaces, scarves, and purses are the same hazard during play and sleep. Safe Kids says to take them off. A helmet used for riding comes off when the ride is over, before sleep.

## Ride-ons and climbing

CPSC’s toy tips say to get safety gear, including helmets, for scooters and other riding toys, and to use that gear every time. A toy helmet is not that gear. Section 4.19 of the toy standard says simulated protective devices, including toy helmets and toy goggles, have to be labeled to show they are not safety protective devices. Buy the helmet that is made for the ride. Leave the costume helmet for dress-up, off the scooter.

Ride-on toys have to meet stability requirements and must not collapse in an overload test. That is the maker’s duty under section 4.15. Section 4.39 addresses openings in handles and steering wheels so a child’s jaw is not trapped. Your checks are the age label, a real helmet when the toy is a scooter or other riding toy, wheels and axles that are still intact, and you in the room. Which ride-on fits which movement, including feet-on-the-floor toys before pedals, is in <Link href="/blog/gross-motor-toys-toddlers">gross motor toys for toddlers</Link>.

Climbing uses the same room. CPSC’s [Anchor It](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/AnchorItgov) campaign says children climb furniture, and that unsecured TVs, furniture, and appliances are a tip-over hazard. Anchor dressers and shelves. Put televisions on furniture made to hold them, and anchor a set that is not mounted to the wall. Take toys and remotes off the top so they are not a reason to climb. A low climber with you close enough to reach the child is the play version. A dresser is not the climber. Tunnels and other toys that enclose a child have to allow ventilation, and the closure may not lock automatically. That is section 4.16. Length and when a tunnel is too much are in the gross-motor guide. Floor games that skip the purchase are in <Link href="/blog/indoor-gross-motor-activities-toddlers">indoor gross motor activities for toddlers</Link>.

## Paint, plastic, and other materials

New children’s products have numeric lead limits. Accessible parts may not contain more than 100 parts per million of total lead. Paint and similar surface coatings on children’s products may not contain more than 90 parts per million (0.009 percent). Those are CPSC’s [total-lead](https://www.cpsc.gov/FAQ/Total-Lead-Content) and [lead-in-paint](https://www.cpsc.gov/FAQ/Lead-in-Paint) rules, at 15 U.S.C. § 1278a and [16 CFR part 1303](https://www.cpsc.gov/FAQ/Lead-in-Paint).

Certain phthalates are also limited. CPSC prohibits children’s toys and child care articles with more than 0.1 percent of listed phthalates in an accessible plasticized component, under [16 CFR part 1307](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/Phthalates). The AAP says to avoid toys with toxic materials and to look for a label that says nontoxic. A sticker is a start. The lead and phthalate limits are the federal checks behind it.

The CDC’s [lead-in-products page](https://www.cdc.gov/lead-prevention/prevention/consumer-products.html), updated 20 August 2025, says not to let children play with recalled toys, toys made before 2009, or vintage and antique products, because older paint and materials may contain more lead. The Consumer Product Safety Improvement Act of 2008 is why that year matters. Lead in plastic has not been banned in every use. The CDC says lead can be used to soften plastic, and that sunlight, air, or detergents can break that bond into dust a child may swallow from their hands. Toys a person brings into the United States from some other countries are more likely to contain lead than toys made in the United States, Canada, or the European Union.

Home test kits do not show how much lead is present, and the CDC says their reliability at low levels has not been determined. A certified laboratory is the product test. If you think a child was exposed, contact the child’s healthcare provider. Most children who are exposed have no symptoms. A blood lead test is how exposure is found. The AAP says the same: if you think a child was exposed to lead in a toy, ask the doctor about a blood test. Material and finish questions for wood you are buying new are in <Link href="/blog/sustainable-educational-toys">what to look for in eco-friendly toys</Link>.

## Age labels

Age recommendations do three jobs. The AAP says they tell you whether the toy has a choking hazard, whether the child can understand how to play with it, and whether it matches that stage. CPSC’s toy tips say to follow the age guidance on the package and to match the toy to the child’s interests and abilities. The same page says toys must have age labels if the toy is a choking hazard for children under 3.

A label of 3+ often means small parts are allowed. [16 CFR 1500.19](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-II/subchapter-C/part-1500/section-1500.19) requires a choking-hazard caution when a toy or game intended for children at least 3 and under 6 includes a small part. Small balls and marbles have their own cautions, including toys for children at least 3 and under 8 that contain them. Any latex balloon carries the balloon caution. If the warning is on the box, believe it. Keep that toy away from children under 3, and away from an older child who still mouths pieces.

A second birthday does not end the under-3 rule. A third birthday does not end it for a child who still puts toys in their mouth, or for a room shared with a younger child. The AAP says many choking incidents happen when an older child gives a smaller toy or a dangerous food to a younger one. Safe Kids says to separate toys by age and to watch small game pieces. What changes on the skill shelf at age 3, once mouthing has actually stopped, is in <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-3-year-olds">educational toys for 3-year-olds</Link>.

## Recalls

Search the toy’s name on the [CPSC recall list](https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls) before it is played with, and again when you are given one secondhand. The AAP says a recalled toy should be removed right away. Recall reasons include lead, choking, and fire. The CDC points families to CPSC.gov or 1-800-638-2772 for recall information. If a toy fails in your house, report it at [SaferProducts.gov](https://www.saferproducts.gov/). CPSC’s toy page asks for that report.

Once a new toy is open, throw the plastic wrapping away immediately. CPSC says the packaging itself becomes a dangerous plaything. Section 4.12 of the toy standard also sets a thickness or perforation requirement for certain plastic film, including packaging. A bag from the store is not a toy.

## Supervision

No label replaces an adult in the room. The AAP says to show a child how to use the toy, and to know what older children are handing over. Safe Kids says to supervise babies and young children when they are eating or playing, and to have them eat sitting down, not while lying down or running. A new ride-on, a climber, and a toy with a battery door are the sessions to stay for.

The supervision study in the battery section is the reason the product still has to be safe when you step into the kitchen. A few minutes out of sight is ordinary. A loose battery or a broken piece should not be waiting in the room when that happens. This checklist is not a developmental exam. If a choking event, a swallowed battery or magnet, or a possible lead exposure is the worry, use the emergency steps above and talk with the child’s doctor.

## Safe storage

[Safe Kids](https://www.safekids.org/tip/choking-and-strangulation-prevention-tips) says to separate toys by age when you put them away, so an older child’s small pieces are not on the toddler’s floor. A bin without a lid is the simple version.

A toy box with a lid is a different product. The AAP’s [toy-box page](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-home/Pages/Toy-Box-Safety.aspx), from *Caring for Your Baby and Young Child* and last updated 2 November 2009, prefers open shelves so a child can reach toys without a lid. If you use a box, the AAP says to choose one with no top, a lightweight removable lid, or sliding panels, and to watch fingers at the edges. A hinged lid needs a support that holds it open at any angle. If it does not have that support, install one or take the lid off. The box needs ventilation holes, or a gap between the lid and the box, that stay open when the box is against a wall. The lid should not latch.

CPSC repeated those hazards in a [2014 warning](https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2014/CPSC-Warns-Consumers-of-the-Deadly-Dangers-of-Storage-and-Toy-Chests) after children were trapped in chests that latched, and after lids fell on necks when a support failed. The steps in that warning: if a lid support does not hold the lid open in every position, remove it or replace it with a spring-loaded support that does. Disable or remove an automatic latch. Keep ventilation holes unblocked by the floor or the wall. The current toy standard still requires toy chests to address pinching, crushing, laceration, and suffocation, which is section 4.41 of CPSC’s business guidance. Open shelves skip that hardware.

## Hand-me-downs

A passed-down toy skips the new-product testing you never see. Walk this list before it goes on the floor.

1. Search the name on the [CPSC recall list](https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls). If it is recalled, it is done. The AAP says to remove it right away.
2. Check the era. The CDC says children should not play with toys made before 2009, or with vintage and antique products. Many used children’s products are exempt from the total-lead ban, except children’s metal jewelry and products the seller knows are over the limit. CPSC states that exemption on its [total-lead page](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Lead/Total-Lead-Content), at 15 U.S.C. § 1278a. Legal to give away is not the same as safe to mouth. Skip the old painted toy.
3. Tug eyes, noses, knobs, wheels, and seams. A part that comes off gets the cylinder test. The AAP wants stuffed toys well made, machine washable when they are fabric, and free of loose ribbons and bean-like pellets.
4. Open the battery door only with the tool it was built for. If it opens without a tool, or a magnet or bead falls out, retire the toy.
5. Look for cracks, splinters, sharp points, and lifted paint. Ordinary wear is how a toy that was safe becomes a small part.
6. Wash what can be washed only after it passes the steps above. Soap and a damp cloth for wood, then a full dry, is the cleaning post. A leaded finish is not a dirt problem.

Children’s metal jewelry is a poor hand-me-down. The CDC says children should not wear jewelry that may contain lead, because they chew and suck it. Safe Kids says to take jewelry off for play and sleep.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are safe toys for toddlers?

Large, sturdy toys intended for children under 3, with no small parts, no loose button battery, no high-powered magnets, and no added strings. The AAP’s buying guide adds a nontoxic label, parts larger than the child’s mouth, and an age label you actually read. The checklist on this page is the pass for about 12 to 36 months.

### How do I check a toy for choking hazards?

Try to fit the toy and every removable piece, without squeezing, into a space 1.25 inches across and 2.25 inches deep. If it disappears inside, it is a small part. Also try a ball in a 1.75-inch hole. A toilet-paper tube is a looser test than the official cylinder, so a piece that does not fit in the tube can still be too small. Follow the age label, and keep a 3+ toy with a choking-hazard warning away from children under 3.

### What should toddlers not have because of button batteries or magnets?

Do not leave them with a toy whose battery door opens without a tool, with loose coin cells, or with high-powered magnet balls, cubes, or desk sets. A swallowed button battery can burn the esophagus in as little as two hours. Swallowed magnets can pinch tissue between them. Both are emergency-department problems, not a wait-and-see problem. The phone numbers are in the battery section.

### Are ride-on toys and climbers safe for ages 1 to 3?

They can be, when the age label matches the child, you are in reach, and a scooter or other riding toy is used with a real helmet every time. A toy helmet does not count as that helmet. Ride-ons have to pass stability and overload tests before they are sold. Climbers stay low. Anchor the furniture in the room, and keep toys off the tops of dressers and TVs so climbing those is less tempting.

### How do I inspect a hand-me-down toy?

Search for a recall, skip toys made before 2009 and anything vintage, then tug every part that could come off. Check the battery door, magnets, cords, cracks, and lifted paint. Used toys are often exempt from the lead ban that applies to new children’s products, except metal jewelry and toys the seller knows are over the limit. That exemption is why the CDC tells you not to rely on age alone. If you think a child was exposed to lead, ask the doctor about a blood test.

### How should toys be stored for a toddler?

On open shelves, or in a bin with no lid, with older siblings’ small pieces stored apart. If a chest has a hinged lid, the lid has to stay open at any angle, the box needs ventilation that a wall does not block, and the lid should not latch. CPSC has warned that a latching chest can trap a child and that a falling lid can injure the neck.

### How is this different from the infant toy guide and the age guides?

The infant guide is the first year: materials, edges, cleaning, and crib toys, and it stops at the first birthday. The age guides sort a shelf by skill for one year, and they send the hazard list here. The puzzle, squishy, cleaning, and gross-motor posts each cover one kind of object. This page is the hazard list for about 12 to 36 months: choking, batteries, magnets, cords, ride-ons, climbing, paint, age labels, recalls, supervision, storage, and hand-me-downs.

## What to try first

Take one toy the child already uses. Run the cylinder check, tug the parts, open nothing that needs a tool unless you are throwing a loose battery away, and search the name on the recall list. If it fails, take it out of the room the same day.

Then use the age page for what to play with next: <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-1-year-olds">about 12 to 24 months</Link> or <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-2-year-olds">about 24 to 36 months</Link>. If the toy is wood and it passed the checks, clean it with the damp-cloth method when it is dirty.
