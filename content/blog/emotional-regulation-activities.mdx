Your preschooler loses a game, shouts, and pushes the pieces away. A lecture about calming down is a lot of words for that moment. This page is the hands-on version: co-regulation, movement, naming a feeling, and quiet calm-down play.

It is not a guide to social-emotional toys in general. Dolls, puppets, and games matched to those broader skills are in <Link href="/blog/social-emotional-learning">social-emotional toys</Link>. Household pretend scenes for about age 3 are in <Link href="/blog/pretend-play-social-skills">pretend play ideas for 3-year-olds</Link>. Toys with one shared goal are in <Link href="/blog/cooperative-play-toys-sharing">cooperative play toys</Link>.

**Quick answer:** Start with your own calm voice and fewer words. For babies and toddlers, stay close, which is the coregulation [NAEYC describes](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/yc/summer2024/rocking-and-rolling). For a 3-year-old, the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html) suggests words for the feeling, a breath, a hug for a favorite toy, or a quiet place. For about ages 4 to 7, [Harvard's activity guide](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/5-Games-Activities-for-Children-4-7-years.pdf) uses a short stop-and-go game such as freeze dance. Learn a new activity while the child is settled. Use the familiar one later.

## Start with co-regulation before asking children to calm themselves

[NAEYC's article on coregulation](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/yc/summer2024/rocking-and-rolling), written with ZERO TO THREE, describes babies as born with little ability to regulate emotional states. From birth to age 3, they depend on adults to soothe them. The article calls the adult's part "sharing our calm," or coregulation: a calm voice, gentle touch, slowed breathing, and a named feeling help the child begin to understand, express, and manage the feeling. It also says adults cannot share calm they do not have. For children 1 year and older, the same article suggests a lovey to cuddle.

That source is about infants and toddlers, not older children who can already use a game. With a baby or toddler, sit close. Name what you see in a few words: "You're disappointed the game ended. I'm staying with you." Slow your own breathing. If they want to be held, hold them. If they push away, stay nearby without crowding.

The CDC's [3-year milestone page](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html), reviewed 16 May 2026, gives the next step once a child can use words. Talk about emotions and give words for the feeling. Help with stressful feelings by teaching a deep breath, a hug for a favorite toy, or a move to a quiet, safe place. Offer one of those. If they refuse the breath, use the hug or the quiet place. Do not insist on a particular way of breathing.

## Match the activity to what the child needs right now

### Try movement games that practise starting and stopping

The Center on the Developing Child at Harvard publishes [games for children ages 4 to 7](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/5-Games-Activities-for-Children-4-7-years.pdf). The guide says executive function, self-regulation, and physical skills develop quickly in those years, and that a game should be challenging without becoming too frustrating. Freeze dance asks children to move, then stop and stay still when the music stops. Simon Says is described as a way to test attention. Red light, green light is listed with it as a similar stop-and-go game.

Use one cue, such as the music stopping or the words "red light." Play a short round. If the child gets louder, faster, or more frantic, the game has become too frustrating. Stop it. Lower your voice and offer sitting together or a quiet building activity.

A second Harvard sheet, [playful activities for ages 8 to 12](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/6-Playful-Activities-for-Kids-8-12-years.pdf), says play can strengthen executive function and self-regulation, and its examples mention memory, mental flexibility, and attention. This page stays with the early-childhood activities above.

### Use pretend play to name a feeling

The same CDC 3-year page says to give children words for emotions, and it suggests role play when a new place or new people are coming up. [NAEYC's article on dramatization in kindergarten](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/yc/summer2024/intentionally-building-self-regulation) describes acting out a story as practice for managing bodies, emotions, and attention, alongside oral language. A short scene at home can use a puppet, a doll, or a stuffed animal. You do not need a scripted lesson.

Try three lines, then follow their play:

- Name what happened: "The bear had to wait for a turn."
- Ask about the character: "How might Bear feel?"
- Try one response: "Could Bear ask for space, take a pause, or find an adult?"

If they only pat the toy, that is enough. Skip the quiz and follow their play. Setups that stay at one prop and one line, for about age 3, are in the pretend-play post. If the problem is sharing one toy rather than settling, use the cooperative-play post.

### Offer calming play that slows the pace

Some children need less noise and fewer instructions. NAEYC's coregulation article lists sensory play that can be calming, including textured toys, sand and water, art, and music, with an adult nearby. A later [NAEYC handout on playful learning](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/eyc/fall2025/message-in-a-backpack) names blocks, water, and playdough as open-ended materials to use together, and it says to follow the child's lead.

Calm-down choices that stay inside those sources:

- Hug a stuffed animal or doll, or move to a quiet spot. Those are the CDC's 3-year suggestions, along with a breath the child can copy.
- Squish play dough. The CDC page suggests squishing, pressing, and pinching playdough at age 3 as hand practice. Treat it as a quiet material you use together, and stop if they refuse it. Foam and gel squeeze toys are a separate safety check, in <Link href="/blog/squishy-toys">squishy toys for kids</Link>.
- Build with a few blocks, or draw one slow line. Ask what they notice, which is the question the playful-learning handout suggests.
- Use a shallow tray of water only while you are within reach. The American Academy of Pediatrics says on [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/Water-Safety-And-Young-Children.aspx) that young children can drown in as little as an inch or two of water, and that open water containers should be emptied after use. Stay within arm's reach. Empty the tray before you leave, including for a minute. One slow breath that pushes a paper boat is enough. Skip the activity if they put materials in their mouth and you cannot watch every piece.

Offer one choice. If they refuse it, stay close. NAEYC's [family handout on stress](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/eyc/summer2025/message-in-a-backpack) says play can include movement and laughter, and that the useful way in is to follow the child's lead during everyday activities.

## Toy ideas by type

These are kinds of materials, not products. Use what you already have. Nothing on this list is a brand, a set to buy, or a rated pick.

| Toy type | Useful when | One way to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Soft doll or stuffed animal | They need something to hold | Offer a hug. NAEYC suggests a lovey from age 1. CDC's 3-year tips include hugging a favorite toy when upset. |
| Puppet | You are naming a feeling through a character | One line from the puppet, then follow their idea. NAEYC's playful-learning handout lists puppets as a warm way to respond. |
| Blocks | The room needs to get quieter | Build a small structure together. Blocks are one of the open-ended materials in that same handout. |
| Play dough | Their hands need something to squeeze | Squish and pinch, with you nearby. Stop if they mouth it or push it away. |
| Shallow water tray | They can slow down and you can stay in reach | One pour, or one breath that moves a paper boat. Empty the tray when you stop. |
| No extra toy | Energy is high, about ages 4 to 7 | Freeze dance or red light, green light, from the Harvard 4-to-7 guide. Stop if it gets too frustrating. |

## Keep a small routine they can remember

A short routine gives you fewer decisions when feelings are high. Use four steps on an ordinary day first, so the choice is familiar later:

1. Notice the feeling and give it a word.
2. Stay close with a calm voice.
3. Choose one material from the list above.
4. Return to the earlier activity only when they are settled.

NAEYC's stress handout suggests making time for play during everyday activities and following the child's lead. Harvard's 4-to-7 guide says younger children in that range may need help learning the rules of a game. Teach freeze dance then: a settled afternoon, one short round, then stop while it is still friendly.

After a sibling argument over one truck, you might say, "You both wanted the truck. We'll pause." Stay nearby, offer the puppet or a few blocks, and talk about sharing only after both children have settled. The turn-taking setup itself is the cooperative-play guide.

## Stop when the activity adds pressure

Stop when the child gets louder or faster, refuses every choice, throws materials, or cannot follow one simple cue.

- Use fewer words.
- Turn off music and move extra toys out of reach.
- Stay close without crowding.
- Offer one option, such as sitting together. NAEYC's coregulation article includes holding or rocking when the child welcomes it.

If freeze dance winds them up, turn the music off and say, "The game is finished. I'm here." Safety comes before finishing the round.

These activities are everyday practice. They do not replace a doctor. The CDC 3-year page says you know your child best, and not to wait if your child is not meeting milestones, has lost skills they once had, or if you have other concerns. Talk with the child's doctor, share what you are seeing, and ask about developmental screening.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are emotional regulation activities for children?

They are short play moments that help a child notice a feeling and choose a next step, with an adult nearby. For babies and toddlers, NAEYC describes that adult role as coregulation. At age 3, the CDC suggests words for the feeling, a deep breath, a hug for a favorite toy, or a quiet place. Around ages 4 to 7, Harvard's guide uses stop-and-go games such as freeze dance.

### When should we practise them?

Use a calm presence in the hard moment. Teach a new game on an ordinary day, while the child is settled, then offer that same choice later. NAEYC's family handout suggests making time for play during everyday activities and following the child's lead. Harvard says a game should be challenging, not too frustrating.

### What if an activity makes things worse?

Stop. Use fewer words, remove extra noise, and stay nearby. Offer sitting together, or holding if the child wants it. If someone could get hurt, deal with that first. If distress is most days, skills have been lost, or you are worried, talk with the child's doctor and ask about developmental screening.

### Do I need to buy a special calm-down set?

No. A doll or stuffed animal, a puppet, blocks, play dough, a shallow tray of water, or a movement game covers this page. Skip anything with tiny pieces if the child still mouths objects.

### How is this different from social-emotional toys?

The social-emotional toys page matches ordinary toys to skills such as naming a feeling, taking a turn, or caring for a doll. This page is the sequence to use when a child needs help settling: co-regulation, movement, naming a feeling, and quiet play.

## What to try first

Pick one activity the child already tolerates. Teach it once today, while things are calm. Put that one material where you can reach it the next time feelings run high, and leave the rest put away.
