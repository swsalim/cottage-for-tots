Gross vs fine motor skills is one of the first comparisons parents hear at a check-up. Gross motor skills are the large movements of the arms, legs, and core, such as crawling, walking, and jumping. Fine motor skills are the small movements of the hands and fingers, such as grasping, drawing, and buttoning.

Children usually become steady with those large movements before the precise ones. The two still grow together. Sitting up makes it easier to pick a block off the floor, and a steady trunk makes it easier to draw.

This guide puts the difference in one place: a side-by-side comparison, the milestones most children reach by each age, and simple play that builds both. For the wider picture of how children grow, see our guide to <Link href="/blog/early-childhood-development">early childhood development</Link>.

## The difference, in one minute

Gross motor skills use the big muscles. They move the whole body, or a large part of it. Rolling, sitting, crawling, walking, running, jumping, and climbing are gross motor skills.

Fine motor skills use the small muscles of the hands and fingers. Holding a rattle, picking up a crumb, scribbling, using a spoon, and buttoning a shirt are fine motor skills.

A quick way to tell them apart: waving an arm is a gross motor skill. Writing with that same arm is a fine motor skill. The shoulder makes the big sweep. The fingers make the small one. [Cleveland Clinic’s patient guide](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/gross-motor-skills), last reviewed in 2023, describes the difference the same way: large muscle groups and large movements, compared with small, precise movements of the wrists, hands, and fingers.

## Gross vs fine motor skills at a glance

| | Gross motor skills | Fine motor skills |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Muscles | Arms, legs, and core | Hands, fingers, and wrists |
| Kind of movement | Large and whole-body | Small and precise |
| Examples | Crawling, walking, running, jumping, climbing | Grasping, drawing, using a spoon, buttoning |
| What it unlocks | Moving around the room and the playground | Self-care, drawing, and early writing |
| Play that helps | Tummy time, balls, climbing, push toys | Rattles, blocks, crayons, beads, playdough |

Many everyday jobs use both columns at once. A toddler who walks to the shelf (gross) and then lifts a cup with their fingers (fine) is using the two skills in one trip.

## What are Gross Motor Skills?

Gross motor skills are the movements children make with the larger muscles of the arms, legs, and trunk. They are how a baby learns to hold their head up, how a toddler learns to walk, and how a preschooler learns to jump and climb.

These skills show up in ordinary play:

- **Rolling and sitting.** A baby turns over, then learns to sit without being held.
- **Crawling and walking.** The child moves across the room, then walks while holding furniture, then walks alone.
- **Running and jumping.** Both feet leave the ground, and the landing stays controlled.
- **Throwing, kicking, and climbing.** The body aims an object, or moves up and over something.

Gross motor skills matter because they let children explore. A child who can sit, crawl, or walk reaches more toys, more people, and more of the room. That exploration is also how strength, balance, and coordination get their practice. ## What are Fine Motor Skills?

Fine motor skills are the movements children make with the smaller muscles of the hands and fingers. They are how a baby holds a toy, how a toddler scribbles, and how a preschooler starts to use a fork or a button.

These skills show up in ordinary tasks:

- **Grasping.** The hand closes around a rattle, then later picks up a small piece of food with the thumb and finger.
- **Drawing.** A crayon starts as a fist-hold and later sits between the fingers and thumb.
- **Self-care.** Spoons, cups, loose clothing, and buttons all ask the fingers to work on purpose.
- **Building and turning.** Stacking blocks, turning a page, and twisting a lid are fine motor work.

Fine motor skills are a large part of getting ready for school, because drawing and early writing depend on them. They are also how children start to dress, eat, and look after themselves. ## How the two skills work together

Gross and fine motor skills are partners. A baby can use their hands to pick a toy off the floor more easily once they can sit. The sitting is gross motor work. The picking up is fine motor work. The first skill makes a stable base for the second.

You can see the same pairing later on:

- Drawing needs a steady trunk, so the shoulder and back hold still while the fingers move.
- Catching a ball needs the arms and eyes working as a team, then the hands closing at the right moment.
- Climbing a step needs the legs, then a hand on the rail for balance.

Toys often ask for both at once. A push-along walker moves the legs and also gives the hands something to grip. A ball is a kick one minute and a two-handed catch the next. ## Milestones by age

The ages below come from the CDC’s “Learn the Signs. Act Early.” milestone checklists, updated in February 2026. The CDC groups these under movement and physical development. The table sorts each skill into the gross or fine column so you can compare them.

In 2026, the CDC describes these as skills that most children, about 75% or more, can do by that age. They are a guide for conversations with your child’s doctor. They are not a screening test, and they are not a full list of everything a child can do. Children move through them at their own pace.

| Age | Gross motor skills | Fine motor skills |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 months | Holds head up when on tummy. Moves both arms and both legs. | Opens hands briefly. |
| 4 months | Holds head steady when held. Pushes up onto elbows on tummy. Swings an arm at a toy. | Holds a toy placed in the hand. Brings hands to mouth. |
| 6 months | Rolls from tummy to back. Pushes up on straight arms. Leans on hands when sitting. | — |
| 9 months | Gets to sitting alone. Sits without support. | Moves a toy from one hand to the other. Rakes food with the fingers. |
| 12 months | Pulls up to stand. Walks while holding furniture. | Picks up small bits of food with thumb and pointer finger. Drinks from a lidless cup while you hold it. |
| 15 months | Takes a few steps alone. | Feeds themself some food with the fingers. |
| 18 months | Walks without holding on. Climbs on and off a couch or chair. | Scribbles. Finger-feeds. Tries a spoon. Drinks from a lidless cup and may spill. |
| 2 years | Kicks a ball. Runs. Walks up a few stairs with or without help. | Eats with a spoon. |
| 30 months | Jumps off the ground with both feet. | Twists lids and doorknobs. Turns book pages one at a time. Takes off some loose clothes. |
| 3 years | — | Strings large beads. Uses a fork. Puts on some loose clothes. |
| 4 years | Catches a large ball most of the time. | Holds a crayon between fingers and thumb. Unbuttons some buttons. Serves food or pours water with an adult nearby. |
| 5 years | Hops on one foot. | Buttons some buttons. |

A dash means the CDC movement checklist for that age does not list a separate skill in that column. Practice is still happening. At 6 months, for example, the CDC lists reaching for a wanted toy under learning and thinking, while the movement list is the large-body skills in the middle column.

Source: [CDC developmental milestones](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html) and the [CDC’s notes on how the checklists were built](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/key-points.html), both updated 16 February 2026.

## Development of Fine Motor Skills

The hand has its own sequence, and it is easy to miss because the steps are small. The CDC movement checklists, updated in 2026, show that sequence in everyday tasks.

Around 2 months, most babies open their hands briefly. By 4 months, most can hold a toy that you place in the hand and can bring their hands to their mouth. The grasp is still a whole-hand hold.

By 9 months, most babies move a toy from one hand to the other and use the fingers to rake food toward themselves. The thumb and finger are starting to work as a pair. By 12 months, most babies pick up small bits of food between the thumb and pointer finger. That pinch is the pincer grasp, and it is what makes finger foods possible.

From there, the hands take on tools. At 15 months, most children finger-feed themselves some food. At 18 months, most scribble and try a spoon. At 2 years, most eat with a spoon. At 30 months, most twist a lid or a doorknob, turn book pages one at a time, and take off some loose clothes.

The school-years grip arrives later. At 3, most children string large beads, use a fork, and put on some loose clothes. At 4, most hold a crayon between the fingers and thumb, not in a fist, and can unbutton some buttons. At 5, most can button some buttons.

You do not need a drill to support this. Offer toys that fit the grasp your child has now: a rattle for a whole-hand hold, blocks for a pincer grasp, and chunky crayons once scribbling starts. <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-infants">Safe toys for infants</Link> covers what to look for while the grasp is still new. <Link href="/blog/toys-improve-focus-concentration">Toys that build focus</Link> covers the quieter, hands-busy play that comes next.

## Activities and toys by age

Play is the practice. A few minutes on the floor, repeated through the week, does more than a single long session. Stay close enough to help, and stop while your child is still enjoying it.

### Babies

Tummy time is the gross motor starting point. Put your baby on their tummy for short spells while you stay beside them, and place a toy just far enough away that they lift their head or push up to look at it. Rolling from tummy to back can be a game: a toy at one side gives them a reason to turn.

For the hands, offer a light rattle or a grasping ring they can hold, mouth, and pass from hand to hand. Bath time works too. A sponge to squeeze and a cup to fill give the fingers a job. Keep infant toys large enough that they cannot be swallowed, and check them the way we describe in the infant toy guide.

### Toddlers

Toddlers want to move. A push toy, a soft ball to roll or kick, and a short path to walk all build balance and leg strength. Walking on grass or a play mat is enough uneven ground for practice. Stairs can wait for a handrail and your hand. Floor games for a rainy day, without climbing the furniture, are in <Link href="/blog/indoor-gross-motor-activities-toddlers">indoor gross motor activities</Link>.

The fine motor play at this age is stacking, posting, and marks on paper. Two blocks become a tower. A shape dropped into a hole becomes a game. A chunky crayon and a large sheet of paper become scribbles. Let them try a spoon at meals, and expect a mess. The mess is the practice. Household versions of those jobs are in <Link href="/blog/fine-motor-activities-toddlers">fine motor activities for toddlers</Link>.

### Preschoolers

Preschoolers can string a few skills into one game. An indoor course made of pillows, a taped line to walk, and a large ball to catch covers balance, running, and catching. Outdoors, a playground with space to climb and run does the same job.

The hands are ready for playdough, large beads, simple puzzles, and child-safe scissors with you beside them. Cooking is useful too: stirring, pouring with you there, and tearing lettuce all ask for a controlled grip. Buttons and zippers on a dress-up shirt turn self-care into play. ## When to talk with a doctor

You know your child best. The CDC’s 2026 guidance is to act early if your child is not meeting one or more milestones, has lost a skill they once had, or if something else concerns you. Talk with your child’s doctor, share what you have noticed, and ask about developmental screening.

In 2026, the CDC notes that the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends general developmental screening at 9, 18, and 30 months, and autism screening at 18 and 24 months, or whenever a parent or doctor has a concern. Screening is a formal check. This article is not that check.

Bring a few notes if you go: the age, the skill you expected, and whether a skill has slipped away. A missing milestone is a reason for a conversation. It is not a diagnosis you need to make at home. If you are unsure, ask sooner. The CDC’s line is the right one: don’t wait.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which develops first, gross or fine motor skills?

Large movements usually become reliable first. Head control, rolling, sitting, and walking settle in before a pencil grip or buttons. The hands are working from the early months too. By 2 months, most babies open their hands briefly, according to the CDC’s 2026 checklist. Cleveland Clinic’s 2023 guide says infants develop gross motor skills before fine motor skills. In daily life you will see both, with the big movements making a stable base for the small ones.

### What is an example of each?

Crawling across the room is a gross motor skill. Picking up a block at the end of that crawl is a fine motor skill. Running is gross. Scribbling is fine. Jumping with both feet is gross. Using a spoon is fine.

### Can toys help motor skills?

Yes. Toys give the muscles a reason to repeat a movement. A ball, a push toy, and room to climb support gross motor skills. Rattles, blocks, crayons, beads, and playdough support fine motor skills. The best toy is one your child can use at their current grasp and balance, with you nearby. Our <Link href="/blog/best-toys-motor-skills">toy guide for motor skills</Link> goes through options by what they train.

### When should I worry about motor skills?

Talk with your child’s doctor if a milestone on the CDC checklist is missing, if your child loses a skill they had already learned, or if you have another concern. Ask about developmental screening. This page can help you notice what you are seeing. Your child’s doctor is the person who can tell you what it means.

## Conclusion

Gross motor skills move the body. Fine motor skills move the hands. Children need both, and they build both through ordinary play: tummy time, a ball, a rattle, a crayon, a spoon, a flight of cushions.

Use the table as a conversation guide, not a score sheet. The CDC’s 2026 checklists describe what most children can do by each age, and they leave room for a child who takes their own time. If a skill is missing or a skill has gone, talk with your child’s doctor.
