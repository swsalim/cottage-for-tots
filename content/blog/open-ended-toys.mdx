An open-ended toy has no single right way to play with it. A block can be a phone, a wall, a cake, or a bridge. A toy fire truck is usually a fire truck. That is the whole test: does the child decide what happens, or does the toy?

This page is the long version. It explains what counts, gives examples for babies, toddlers, and preschoolers (including about ages 1, 2, 3, and 4), and shows how to spot one on a shelf or a product page. A short section sits inside the <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-child-development">guide to choosing educational toys</Link>. How to put one object out and then stop directing is in <Link href="/blog/creative-play-grow">creative play at home</Link>.

**Quick answer:** An open-ended toy can be used in many ways, and the child supplies the story. Blocks, nesting cups, scarves, dolls, play food, sand tools, and a cardboard box are common types. Closed-ended toys, such as a puzzle, still belong on the shelf. Check piece size before anything reaches a child under 3.

## What makes a toy open-ended

NAEYC, the National Association for the Education of Young Children, puts it simply in [Five Essentials to Meaningful Play](https://www.naeyc.org/our-work/families/five-essentials-meaningful-play): open-ended materials can be used in many ways, so children decide for themselves how to use them. Its example is a block that a child imagines as a fire truck, next to a toy fire truck that is usually only a fire truck.

A second NAEYC piece, [Play with Free Stuff: No Batteries Required!](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/blog/play-free-stuff-no-batteries-required) (Toni Denese Sturdivant, 1 October 2018), makes the same point with cardboard. A toy phone with buttons is good at being a phone. A rectangle of cardboard can be a phone, a remote, a boat, a doll’s bed, or a shopping list.

[ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/tips-for-choosing-toys-for-toddlers-toddler-toys/) (updated 27 April 2025) uses the same word. It says to choose toys that are open-ended, meaning you can play many different games with them, such as wooden blocks that become a road, a zoo, a bridge, or a spaceship. Its examples are blocks, stacking cups, and sand and water play. The same page says the more a toy does on its own, the less the child has to do.

Three questions sort most toys. They are a home test, drawn from those descriptions, not a score from a study.

1. **Is there a finish line?** A shape sorter is done when every shape is in. A pile of blocks is never done.
2. **Who supplies the story?** A toy that talks, sings, and lights up on its own supplies most of it. A doll and a blanket leave the story to the child.
3. **Does it still work as the child changes?** ZERO TO THREE tells families to look for toys that stay fun at different stages. A 2-year-old stacks blocks. A 4-year-old builds a road, then a zoo, then compares two towers.

Closed-ended toys still earn a place. Puzzles, sorters, and lacing cards teach a clear skill and give a child the satisfaction of finishing. The <Link href="/blog/toys-improve-focus-concentration">concentration toys</Link> page uses that finish line on purpose, and <Link href="/blog/wooden-puzzles-for-toddlers">wooden puzzles for toddlers</Link> is the puzzle version. A good shelf has both.

Whether a toy is Montessori-aligned is a different question, about one clear idea and a child who can put the material back. That test is in <Link href="/blog/montessori-toys">Montessori toys</Link>.

## What the evidence says, and what it does not

There is no study proving that “open-ended toys” as a category make children smarter. ZERO TO THREE says the research does not support packaging that claims a toy will make a baby smarter. HealthyChildren.org’s [summary of the American Academy of Pediatrics toy report](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/pages/What-to-Look-for-in-a-Toy.aspx) says a certain toy is not necessary for the next milestone. The evidence below is about the kinds of play these materials invite.

**Fewer toys, deeper play.** In a lab study of 36 toddlers, children given 4 toys had fewer play incidents, longer stretches with a toy, and more ways of playing than when given 16 toys ([Dauch and colleagues, Infant Behavior and Development, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29190457/)). The abstract says that longer time with one toy can leave room to explore and play more creatively. It does not name a correct number of toys for a home. How a small set can be swapped is in <Link href="/blog/toy-rotation">how to set up a toy rotation</Link>.

**Block building and early math.** In a study of 102 three-year-olds, spatial assembly with interlocking block constructions independently predicted a significant amount of the variability in their concurrent math scores ([Verdine and colleagues, Child Development, 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24112041/)). That is a link between a skill and a score at the same age. It is not proof that buying blocks raises math scores. It is a reason to keep blocks on the floor.

**Electronic toys and talk.** In a study of 26 parent-infant pairs, infants were 10 to 16 months old. Play with electronic toys came with fewer adult words, fewer conversational turns, fewer parent responses, and fewer content-specific words than play with books or traditional toys ([Sosa, JAMA Pediatrics, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26720437/)). Books drew the most adult words. The paper’s conclusion is that electronic-toy play should be discouraged when the goal is early language, and that traditional toys are a useful alternative when reading is not the activity.

**What pediatricians recommend.** The American Academy of Pediatrics clinical report [Selecting Appropriate Toys for Young Children in the Digital Era](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30509931/) (Healey and Mendelsohn, Pediatrics, 2019) encourages caregiver play that is rich in language, pretending, problem-solving, and creativity. The HealthyChildren.org summary says the skills children need for school, such as impulse control, managing emotions, and flexible thinking, are best learned through unstructured and social play. It also says the best toys match the skills a child has now.

## Open-ended toy examples by age

Ages are a guide. NAEYC’s [toys by age and stage](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/topics/play/toys) says items for one age, as long as they are safe, can suit children younger or older. The CDC milestone lists describe what most children do. They are not a shopping schedule. The [key points](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/key-points.html) page says the milestones were placed where about 75% or more of children would be expected to show them, and that the checklists are not a screening test.

| Age | Open-ended examples | What the child does with them | Safety check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Older infants, about 7 to 12 months | Large soft blocks, wooden cubes, nesting toys, plastic bowls | Drop, bang, fill, empty, peek | Every piece too big for the small-parts cylinder |
| 1-year-olds | Wood or cardboard blocks about 2 to 4 inches, a doll, scarves, a cardboard box | Stack, carry, pretend to feed, crawl in | Same cylinder rule; no loose eyes or buttons |
| 2-year-olds | More blocks, sand and water tools, dress-up clothes, play food, chunky crayons | Build, pour, dress up, put toy food on a plate | Still under 3: the cylinder rule applies |
| 3 to 5 years | Many blocks for big builds, construction sets, playdough and tools, collage scraps, puppets | Plan, build together, tell stories, pretend to be someone else | Check magnets and small parts if a younger sibling plays nearby |

The baby and 1-year-old rows follow NAEYC. For older infants, about 7 to 12 months, it lists large soft blocks, wooden cubes, nesting toys, and plastic bowls, plus baby dolls and puppets for pretend. For 1-year-olds it lists cardboard and wood blocks of about 2 to 4 inches, dolls, dress-up scarves, and puppets. For 2-year-olds it lists blocks, construction sets, play food, dress-up clothes, sand and water toys, and large crayons. For 3- to 6-year-olds it lists many blocks for complex structures, construction sets, playdough and modeling tools, and paper and cloth scraps for collage.

The CDC [2-year tips](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html) (reviewed 16 May 2026) suggest blocks, dress-up clothes, crayons or finger paint, and sand toys or plastic containers in the tub or a sandbox. By 2 years, most children play with more than one toy at a time, such as putting toy food on a toy plate. By 30 months, most use things to pretend, such as feeding a block to a doll. That line is on the CDC [30-month checklist](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/30-months.html), also reviewed 16 May 2026. By 4 years, most pretend to be something else during play, such as a teacher, a superhero, or a dog, on the CDC [4-year checklist](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html), reviewed 16 May 2026. The CDC [4-year tips](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html) suggest dress-up clothes, pots and pans for pretend cooking, and blocks.

Skill-by-skill shelves are in <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-1-year-olds">educational toys for 1-year-olds</Link>, <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-2-year-olds">educational toys for 2-year-olds</Link>, <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-3-year-olds">educational toys for 3-year-olds</Link>, and <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-4-year-olds">educational toys for 4-year-olds</Link>.

## Free open-ended toys you already have

NAEYC’s free-stuff list includes paper towel rolls, boxes, egg cartons, and plastic bottles. Its 2022 family piece, [Harnessing the Joy of Open-Ended Materials](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/tyc/summer2022/message-backpack-open-ended-materials) (Jenna Valasek, Teaching Young Children, Summer 2022), adds blocks, paint, sand, water, playdough, and natural materials such as twigs and flowers. The CDC [30-month tips](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/30-months.html) say to let a child play with a large box and pretend it is a car, a bus, a train, or a house, and to encourage free play in which they use things in new ways.

A few that work across ages:

- **A large cardboard box on the floor.** A house, a car, a boat, a stage. Keep plastic bags out of it and off it. A box on a chair, or a blanket over furniture, can tip. Floor play is the setup in <Link href="/blog/creative-play-grow">creative play</Link>.
- **Kitchen cups and bowls.** Fill, nest, stack, pretend to cook. NAEYC lists plastic bowls for older infants.
- **Paper and crayons.** Process art ideas are in <Link href="/blog/art-activities-preschoolers-household-items">art activities with household items</Link>.
- **Sand or water tools you already own.** Stay for water. The rest of a water setup is in <Link href="/blog/sensory-play-activities-toddlers">sensory play for toddlers</Link>.

Check size before you hand anything over. Plastic bottle caps are on NAEYC’s list of preschool sorting collections, and an egg carton is on the free-stuff list. Caps, buttons, and pebbles fit the small-parts test. They do not belong with a child under 3 or a child who still mouths toys. If you use a plastic bottle, take the cap off and put the cap away.

## How to spot an open-ended toy before you buy

Product pages rarely say “open-ended.” Match what they show to the descriptions above.

**Good signs**

- The photos show the same pieces built or used several different ways. That is NAEYC’s “many ways” test.
- There are many identical or similar pieces, such as blocks, tiles, or cups. ZERO TO THREE’s examples work this way.
- The toy waits for the child. ZERO TO THREE says that if a child can sit and watch the toy perform, it is more entertaining than useful, and that lights or music with no clear button make cause and effect harder to see.
- The age range spans more than one stage, which is the “grows with them” idea on the same ZERO TO THREE page. Still read the age label for small parts.

**Warning signs**

- One finished model on the box, and the pieces only make that model. That is the toy fire truck in NAEYC’s example: it is usually only itself.
- The toy talks, sings, or lights up on a timer, so the child is the audience. Sosa’s study is the language finding for electronic toys with infants.
- A tablet, computer game, or app whose main selling word is “educational.” HealthyChildren.org says to use caution there. Most of those products target memory skills such as letters and shapes, and the summary says impulse control, managing emotions, and flexible thinking are better learned through unstructured and social play. The word on a wooden block set is a different claim. Judge the object, not the adjective.

## How to play with open-ended toys

The toy is half of it. NAEYC’s 2022 family piece suggests following your child’s lead, narrating what they are doing, playing alongside without building a complete model for them, breaking a big idea into smaller steps when they get frustrated, and asking them to tell you about their work rather than asking “what is it?”

That is the kind of back-and-forth the AAP report describes. You do not need a lesson plan. Sit on the floor, say what you see (“you made it taller”), and wait. One object, and when to stop talking, is in <Link href="/blog/creative-play-grow">creative play</Link>. Ideas for pretend scenes at about age 3 are in <Link href="/blog/pretend-play-social-skills">pretend play</Link>. A building question such as “what keeps the tower standing?” is in <Link href="/blog/preschool-stem-activities-at-home">preschool STEM activities</Link>.

## Safety notes

Open-ended sets tend to have many pieces, so check them like any toy for a young child. The shop checklist for about ages 1 to 3 is in <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-for-toddlers">safe toys for toddlers</Link>. The first year is in <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-infants">safe toys for infants</Link>.

- **Small parts.** Toys intended for children under 3 cannot contain parts that fit entirely in the [small-parts cylinder](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/Small-Parts-for-Toys-and-Childrens-Products), 1.25 inches across and 2.25 inches deep. The test is [16 CFR 1501.4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-II/subchapter-C/part-1501/section-1501.4). Loose parts from an older child’s set count when a younger child is on the same floor.
- **Magnets.** The CPSC [magnets page](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Magnets) says swallowed high-powered magnets can attract each other inside the body, and many incidents have needed surgery. It also says those magnets are much stronger than magnets used in toys. Skip loose high-powered magnet balls and cubes. On a magnetic building toy, throw away any piece with a cracked casing or a loose magnet.
- **Wear and tear.** NAEYC says a once-safe toy can become hazardous with normal wear, so check toys often. Cleaning steps for wood are in <Link href="/blog/clean-wooden-toys">how to clean wooden toys</Link>.

## Open-ended toy ideas by type

These are types, not products. No brand, price, or rating belongs in this list. A later edit can place one specific pick in a row after the safety checks above. Buy a type your child keeps reaching for and you do not already have. If they already have blocks, more blocks often beat a new kind of toy.

| Toy type | Ages it suits | Ways to play |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Large soft blocks | Older infants and young toddlers | Stack, knock down, crawl over |
| Wooden unit blocks | 1 year and up, in a size that passes the cylinder test | Towers, roads, enclosures, pretend food |
| Nesting or stacking cups | Older infants to about 3 | Nest, stack, pour, pretend drinks |
| Play silks or large scarves | 1 year and up | Peekaboo, dress-up, capes, roofs |
| Doll with a blanket | 1 year and up | Feed, rock, put to bed, take to the doctor |
| Play kitchen food and pots | 2 years and up | Cook, shop, serve, sort |
| Sand and water tools | 2 years and up, with an adult at the water | Pour, scoop, build, sink or float |
| Magnetic building tiles | Preschoolers, and only if the age label fits this child | Shapes, houses, ramps; retire a cracked piece |
| Large interlocking bricks | 2 years and up when every piece passes the cylinder test | Copy a model, then invent |
| Playdough with simple tools | About 3 years and up | Roll, cut, pretend bakery |
| Puppets | 2 years and up, with no loose eyes or buttons | Stories, feelings, taking turns |

## Frequently asked questions

### What are open-ended toys?

Toys with no single right way to play. Blocks, dolls, scarves, art supplies, and sand tools are common examples. The child decides what the toy becomes. NAEYC’s test is the block that can be a fire truck, next to a toy fire truck that is usually only a fire truck.

### What are some examples of open-ended toys for toddlers?

For about ages 1 and 2: large blocks, nesting cups, a doll and a blanket, scarves, play food, dress-up clothes, chunky crayons, and a cardboard box. Piece size still has to pass the small-parts cylinder until the third birthday, and for any child who still mouths toys.

### What about open-ended toys for 3- and 4-year-olds?

NAEYC’s 3- to 6-year list adds more blocks for bigger builds, construction sets, playdough, collage scraps, and puppets. By 4, the CDC says most children pretend to be someone else during play. Keep small parts and loose magnets away if a younger sibling shares the floor.

### Are puzzles open-ended?

No. A puzzle has one finished picture. That is useful. Puzzles teach persistence and give a clear end. Keep some closed-ended toys alongside the open-ended ones. How to choose the puzzle is in <Link href="/blog/wooden-puzzles-for-toddlers">wooden puzzles for toddlers</Link>.

### Are open-ended toys better than electronic toys?

For parent-infant talk, the Sosa study found fewer adult words and fewer conversational turns during electronic-toy play than during play with books or traditional toys. Books drew the most talk. A traditional toy the child controls can still have a place. One button the child presses is a different object from a toy that performs on a timer.

### At what age do open-ended toys start?

NAEYC’s older-infant list, about 7 to 12 months, already includes large soft blocks, nesting toys, and bowls. What changes with age is the size of the pieces and how complex the play gets. A toy from another age list can still fit when it is safe.

## What to try first

Pick one open-ended material you already own, such as blocks, cups, or a big box. Put it out with nothing else nearby. Sit with your child and only describe what they do. Next time, add one more material and see if they combine the two. If the floor is already crowded, store the rest first, using the rotation steps.
