A toy builds a motor skill when it asks for a movement the child is already trying. Gross motor toys ask the whole body to roll, walk, kick, climb, or ride. Fine motor toys ask the hands to grasp, place, string, or draw. This page is the map. The longer guides are linked from each row.

The difference between the two skills, and how they show up together, is in <Link href="/blog/gross-fine-motor-skills">gross and fine motor skills</Link>. Toddler movement toys are in <Link href="/blog/gross-motor-toys-toddlers">gross motor toys for toddlers</Link>. Hand practice with cups and paper is in <Link href="/blog/fine-motor-activities-toddlers">fine motor activities for toddlers</Link>. The preschool shelf is in <Link href="/blog/fine-motor-toys-preschoolers">fine motor toys for preschoolers</Link>.

**Quick answer:** Pick the movement first, then the age. Under 3, every separate piece has to stay out of the small-parts cylinder, 1.25 inches across and 2.25 inches deep. A push toy, a large ball, a few blocks, and something to grasp will cover more of the early years than a set of ten special toys. Stay nearby. If a skill has dropped off, or you are worried, ask the child's doctor about developmental screening.

## Match the toy to the movement

Gross motor skills use the larger muscles of the trunk, arms, and legs. [Cleveland Clinic's patient guide](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/gross-motor-skills), last reviewed in 2023, describes them as the big movements: rolling, sitting, crawling, walking, running, jumping. Fine motor skills are the small, precise movements of the wrists, hands, and fingers: grasping, stacking, using a spoon, drawing.

Children usually steady the big movements before the precise ones. The two still share a task. Walking to the shelf is gross motor work. Lifting the cup off it is fine motor work. A toy that only lights up and plays a song asks for neither.

Healey and Mendelsohn, in [Selecting Appropriate Toys for Young Children in the Digital Era](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30509931/) (*Pediatrics*, 2019), say the evidence that toys support development comes mainly from play with a caregiver. The useful toy is one you will get on the floor with. It does not have to be expensive, and it does not have to be new.

Seattle Children's [toy guide for 1- and 2-year-olds](https://www.seattlechildrens.org/health-safety/parenting/toys-and-play-1-and-2-year-olds/) describes the same split in plain language. One-year-olds dump, push, pull, and stack, and they play mostly alone. Two-year-olds climb, kick a larger ball, and push themselves on a wheeled toy. Manipulative toys on that list include nesting cups, shape sorters, pegs, and large lacing beads, with solid blocks and lacing cards for older toddlers.

## How to use one toy this week

Pick one cell in the table. Leave the other toys in the cupboard. <Link href="/blog/toy-rotation">Toy rotation</Link> is the system for that cupboard.

**A ball.** At 1, roll it. At 2, the CDC suggests kicking, rolling, and throwing. At 4, catching a large ball is a milestone. Stand close enough that the catch is likely. One miss, then a shorter throw. Stop while it is still a game.

**Blocks.** At 1, the job is "in the cup." At 2, take turns building a tower and knocking it down. That turn-taking is on the CDC 2-year tip sheet. At 4, blocks are one of the imagination toys the CDC lists beside dress-up clothes and a pot for pretend cooking.

**Beads, lacing, and dough.** Stringing large items is a 3-year milestone. Lacing cards and jumbo beads show up on the Seattle Children's list for older toddlers, and again on therapy gift guides for preschool hands. Playdough at 3, squished and pinched, is the CDC's suggestion for the muscles used later in writing and buttons. Household versions of the same hand work are in the fine-motor activities guide, with choking notes for children who still mouth objects.

**A ride-on.** Foot-to-floor ride-ons, balance bikes, tricycles, helmets, and where to ride are in <Link href="/blog/childrens-riding-toys">children's riding toys</Link>. Seattle Children's puts a ride-on moved by the feet, without steering, with older toddlers, and a small tricycle with a helmet from about 2 and a half to 3 years. Do not start with a balance disc or a pedal bike because a list called it a motor toy.

**Indoors, on a small floor.** A cleared room and fifteen ways to move without a new toy are in <Link href="/blog/indoor-gross-motor-activities-toddlers">indoor gross motor activities</Link>. That page also says what not to climb.

## Safety checks before it comes out

Motor play is where small parts, strings, magnets, and ride-ons cause injuries. Use the checks you already have.

- **Small parts.** Toys intended for children under 3 that fit in the [small-parts cylinder](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/Small-Parts-for-Toys-and-Childrens-Products) are banned. The cylinder is 1.25 inches across and 2.25 inches deep. Anything that fits entirely, without squeezing, stays off the floor if the child still mouths toys. Large beads pass more often than craft beads. Small interlocking bricks wait.
- **Everything else that is easy to miss.** Button batteries, loose magnets, cords, balloons, and recalled toys are in <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-for-toddlers">safe toys for toddlers</Link>. The first year is in <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-infants">safe toys for infants</Link>.
- **Puzzles.** Knob and chunky pieces, and when to add more, are in <Link href="/blog/wooden-puzzles-for-toddlers">wooden puzzles for toddlers</Link>.
- **Scissors and crayons.** The CDC 4-year list includes a crayon held between the fingers and thumb. Safety scissors are a preschool tool, used with you at the table, not a toddler toy. The preschool fine-motor guide covers drawing and cutting sets.

Seattle Children's also says to skip toys with small parts, sharp edges, magnets, and button batteries a child can reach. Put those away before you demonstrate the "fun" one.

## When play is not enough

Some children are slower to run, catch, or use a spoon, and then they get there. Some need a closer look. The CDC says not to wait if a child is missing a milestone, has lost a skill, or if you have another concern. Talk with the doctor and ask about developmental screening. The ages and the script for that conversation are on the milestones page.

A toy list cannot tell you which child is which. If everyday tasks such as climbing stairs, using a cup, or scribbling are much harder than the CDC examples for the age, bring a specific example to the visit. "She cannot kick a ball yet, and she is two and a half" is more useful than "I think she is behind."

## Frequently asked questions

### What toys help motor skills?

Toys that require a movement the child is practicing. Push toys, balls, and ride-ons work the whole body. Blocks, large beads, dough, and crayons work the hands. The CDC tip sheets and Seattle Children's toddler guide both use those kinds of objects. A toy that plays music for the child does not substitute for either list.

### Do I need a set of ten?

No. One whole-body toy and one hand toy, swapped when interest drops, cover the week. Add a third only when the first two are being used. The age table above is a menu, not a shopping list.

### When can children use small building bricks?

After pieces no longer fail the small-parts test for that child. Under 3, small bricks are the wrong size. Large bricks and wooden blocks do the same stacking job. The preschool fine-motor guide is the page for lacing, pegs, and construction sets once the hands and the piece size are ready.

### Are balance toys worth it?

A ball, a push toy, and later a balance bike or tricycle with a helmet practice balance in a way a child can use outside. A balance disc is optional equipment for an older child who already moves well, and it needs you next to it. It is not the first gross-motor toy.

### How is this different from the gross-motor and fine-motor guides?

This page chooses between them. The gross-motor guide is push toys, balls, tunnels, and climbers in more detail. The fine-motor guides are the hand activities and the preschool toys. The comparison of the two skills, milestone by milestone, is the gross-versus-fine page.

## What to try first

Look at what your child tried today: pulling up, kicking, stacking, or scribbling. Put out one toy that repeats that movement. Get on the floor for ten minutes. Put the rest away, including any piece that fits in the small-parts cylinder.
