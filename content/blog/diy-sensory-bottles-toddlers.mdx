A sensory bottle for a toddler is a clear plastic bottle, partly filled with water, with a screw cap you have taped so it will not turn. The child shakes it or rolls it. You stay in the room. Glitter, rice, and water beads are the fillings to leave out, including when the lid looks tight.

This page is for about 12 to 36 months. A preschooler can use the same bottles. The one-bottle version already sits inside <Link href="/blog/sensory-play-activities-toddlers">sensory play activities for toddlers</Link>. Here the job is the seal, and the few fills that are still safe if the tape fails.

Watching a bottle can be a quiet minute. It is not a treatment for anxiety, sleep, or a sensory disorder. If those are the worry, talk with your child’s doctor. Toys chosen for attention, and the limits of that idea, are in <Link href="/blog/toys-improve-focus-concentration">toys for concentration</Link>.

## Seal it before they hold it

Use a clear plastic bottle with a one-piece screw cap. Skip glass. Skip any lid with a straw, a silicone spout, or a push-pull sports cap. Those extra pieces come off. In 2019 the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission recalled about 5.7 million [Contigo kids’ water bottles](https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls/2019/Contigo-Recalls-57-Million-Kids-Water-Bottles-Due-to-Choking-Hazard) because the clear silicone spout could detach. The agency’s notice includes reports of spouts found in children’s mouths. A plain screw cap does not have that spout.

1. Wash the bottle and cap. Rinse until you cannot smell soap. Soap left inside is what they taste if the seal ever fails.
2. Fill it at the sink, with you holding it. Stop around halfway. The air space is what lets the water move. Empty the sink when you are done. The American Academy of Pediatrics says on [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/Water-Safety-And-Young-Children.aspx) that young children can drown in as little as an inch or two of water. A sealed bottle is not a tub. An open bottle, a full sink, and a leak on the floor still need you there.
3. Add at most one object. It has to be too large to fit entirely in the [small-parts test](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/Small-Parts-for-Toys-and-Childrens-Products): a cylinder 1.25 inches across and 2.25 inches deep. If the object only fits in the bottle because you forced it, leave it out. Water alone is a finished bottle.
4. Screw the cap on until it stops. Dry the neck. Wrap duct tape or cloth tape around the cap and the neck so the cap cannot twist. Cover the top of the cap as well.
5. Try to open it yourself. If the cap moves, add tape or throw the bottle out. Hand it over only after it fails your twist test.
6. Stay nearby. If they pick at the tape, chew the cap, or bang it on a table edge, take it back.

Do the taping before they are reaching for it. Super glue and a hot glue gun are the wrong tools for this job. Glue bonds skin, and a glue gun burns. Tape is the seal.

If the water turns cloudy, smells off, or you cannot remember when you filled it, empty the bottle at the sink while the child is in another room. Wash it and retape, or throw it away. A crack, a leak, or tape that has lifted means the trash, not a repair in front of them.

## Six fills that survive an open cap

| Bottle | What you put in | What they can notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plain water | Water, about halfway | Slosh, light, weight |
| One toy | Water and one large plastic toy | The toy moving past the water |
| One color | Water and one drop of washable paint | Color through the plastic |
| Cold and room | Two plain bottles | One feels cooler on the outside |
| Heavy and light | One with water, one empty and taped | Which hand drops |
| Find the toy | The toy bottle, played a second way | Turning until the toy shows |

Every row uses the same cap and the same tape. None of them need a new kind of lid.

### 1. Plain water

**Materials:** the washed bottle, water, tape.

**Try it:** Halfway full. Cap. Tape. Twist-test. Hand it over. They can shake it once or watch the water line.

**What you might see:** One shake, then they drop it. That is a complete try. Offer it again later rather than adding glitter so they stay interested.

**Easier:** You hold the bottle and tip it slowly. They watch.

### 2. Water and one large toy

**Materials:** the same bottle, plus one plastic toy that does not fit in the small-parts cylinder and that drops through the neck without forcing.

**Try it:** Toy in, water halfway, seal, test. Roll it on a clear patch of floor, away from stairs. This is the bottle in the sensory-play list. The floor roll is the gross-motor use. Bigger floor games are in <Link href="/blog/indoor-gross-motor-activities-toddlers">indoor gross motor activities</Link>.

**Skip:** Anything you had to squeeze through the neck. A narrow mouth is a reason to use water only.

### 3. One drop of color

**Materials:** a drop of washable paint labeled for children. Not a cup of paint, and not glitter mixed in.

**Try it:** Drop it in the water, cap the bottle, tip it once so the color spreads, then tape. The color is for looking through plastic. It is not food. If you are not willing to throw the bottle out the moment the tape lifts, skip the paint and stay with clear water.

**What you might see:** The color sticks to the plastic in streaks. That is fine. Do not reopen it to “mix it better.”

### 4. Cold and room temperature

**Materials:** two plain-water bottles, sealed the same way.

**Try it:** Fill one with cold tap water and one with water that has been sitting out. No hot water, and nothing from the stove. They hold one in each hand, on the outside of the bottle. Ask which feels cooler. Then you put both away.

**Easier:** You hold the cold one against the back of their hand for a second, then stop.

**Skip:** A bottle of warm water “to soothe.” Warm water cools off, and a too-hot bottle burns through plastic.

### 5. Heavy and light

**Materials:** one bottle halfway full, one empty bottle taped shut so the caps match.

**Try it:** One bottle in each hand. Ask which arm goes down. Trade hands once, in case the first answer was the stronger arm. For a child closer to 3 or 4, the same comparison shows up as a question in <Link href="/blog/preschool-stem-activities-at-home">preschool STEM activities</Link>. Here the job is the feel, not a lesson.

**Easier:** You hold both. They push the heavier one.

### 6. Find the toy

**Materials:** the toy bottle from activity 2. No new filling.

**Try it:** Turn it until the toy hides. Hand it over. They turn it until the toy shows. One hide is enough. This is a looking game with a finish. It is still not a treatment for attention.

**Easier:** The toy starts in view. They tip the bottle and watch it move.

**Harder:** They hide it for you, and you find it. Then the bottle goes on a shelf they cannot climb.

## What to leave out of the bottle

Water beads do not go in, even “just this once” with a tight lid. The [CPSC safety alert](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/SafetyAlert_5101_WaterBeads_082024.pdf) says the way to deal with them around children 3 and younger is to remove them. Dry beads are easy to lose, look like candy, and can keep growing after they are swallowed. A bottle that opens puts them in the room.

Leave out glitter, sequins, beads, buttons, dry rice, beans, pasta, and sand. Those are the pieces a toddler mouths, and they are the pieces a failed lid dumps on the floor. The same rule is on the <Link href="/blog/montessori-activities-2-year-olds">Montessori activities</Link> page: large objects, or no loose pieces at all.

Leave out oil, hair gel, glycerin, and essential oils. Oil spoils and smears when a bottle leaks. Oils and gels are not a toy filling, and they are a poor thing to have in a mouth or an eye.

Leave out loose magnets. The CPSC [magnet page](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Magnets) says high-powered magnets are a risk from toddler age through the teen years. Two or more swallowed magnets can attract through tissue. A bottle is not a safe place to store them.

Leave out glass, sipper spouts, and sports caps. Leave out super glue and hot glue.

## If they only want to take it apart

That is a normal toddler plan. The bottle was interesting because it was closed. Take it away when the tape is the game, empty it later, and offer a different sense from the sensory-play list: cloth, a pot drum, or a wet sponge.

A bottle is a bad fit for a child who throws hard objects at windows. Roll it on the rug with you, or skip it. The empty plastic bottle, cap taped, is the light version if a full one is too exciting to throw.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do you make a sensory bottle for a toddler?

Wash a clear plastic bottle, fill it about halfway with water, screw on a one-piece cap, and wrap tape around the cap and the neck until you cannot twist it open. Add one large toy only if it cannot fit in the small-parts cylinder. Then stay while they shake or roll it.

### What can you put in a sensory bottle?

Water. A single drop of washable paint. One large plastic toy. A second bottle that is empty, cold, or clear, so they can compare. The list above is the whole set.

### Are glitter bottles safe if the lid is glued?

Glue fails, and toddlers work at lids. When glitter gets out, it is in eyes and mouths, and the pieces are small. This page uses water and tape instead. There is no glue recipe here.

### Do sensory bottles calm toddlers down?

A short look at a moving toy can be a quiet pause. That is not the same as treating a meltdown, anxiety, or a sensory condition. If the bottle helps them settle for a minute, use it as play. If you need it to manage distress every day, talk with their doctor.

### How long does the water stay usable?

Until it clouds, smells, leaks, or you have lost track of when you filled it. Then empty it away from the child and wash the bottle, or throw it away. Do not top it up in their hands.

### Can a preschooler have a different bottle?

They can play the “find the toy” and “which is heavier” versions longer. The filling rules stay the same. Age 4 does not make rice, glitter, or water beads a good idea inside a bottle they can open.

## What to try first

Wash one clear bottle, fill it halfway, tape the screw cap, and try to open it yourself. If you cannot twist it, hand it over for one shake. If you can twist it, it is not ready.
