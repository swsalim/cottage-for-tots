A baby activity center is a place for a baby who can hold their head up and is not walking yet. The name covers more than one object. The one safety rules are written for is a seated stationary activity center: a freestanding product that stays put, surrounds the baby’s torso, and lets them rock, spin, bounce, or step in a small range. Parents also call that object an entertainer or an exercise saucer. An activity table and an activity cube are different. The baby stands at a table or sits beside a cube. Those do not hold the baby in a surrounded seat.

This page is that equipment. A play mat and a play gym are floor setups for a baby who is lying down. How to choose one, and how to do tummy time, is in <Link href="/blog/baby-play-mat">how to choose a baby play mat</Link>. The general check for any infant toy, including small parts, is in <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-infants">safe toys for infants</Link>. Once a baby is pulling up and cruising, the movement toys are in <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-1-year-olds">educational toys for 1-year-olds</Link> and <Link href="/blog/gross-motor-toys-toddlers">gross motor toys for toddlers</Link>.

**Quick answer:** Skip any seat on wheels. The American Academy of Pediatrics says those walkers are unsafe. A stationary center with no wheels is the alternative the AAP names, and only while you are watching. Use it for a baby who can hold their head up, for a short supervised stay, and stop when they can walk or when they reach the weight and height printed on it. Most of awake play still belongs on the floor.

## What it is, and what it is not

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission defines a stationary activity center as a freestanding product meant to remain stationary, so a sitting or standing baby whose torso is completely surrounded can walk, rock, play, spin, or bounce within a limited range of motion. CPSC says these products are intended for a child who can hold their head up unassisted and who cannot walk yet. That definition is on the [CPSC stationary activity center page](https://www.cpsc.gov/FAQ/Stationary-Activity-Centers). The [2019 CPSC rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2019/06/18/2019-12804/safety-standard-for-stationary-activity-centers) says the definition does not include doorway jumpers. This page does not cover doorway jumpers.

CPSC sorts the seated product into three types, from ASTM F2012, the standard incorporated in [16 CFR part 1238](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-II/subchapter-B/part-1238):

- An **open-base** center lets the baby’s feet touch the floor.
- A **closed-base** center does not let the feet touch the floor in any position the manufacturer recommends.
- A **spring-supported** center holds the seat or platform on springs, or on something that acts like springs, from below or from above.

[HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/english/ages-stages/baby/pages/movement-8-to-12-months.aspx), in the AAP’s chapter on movement from 8 to 12 months, updated 7 April 2021, calls this “a stationary exercise saucer or activity center, with a rotating and bouncy seat but no wheels.” The AAP’s walker page, updated 15 August 2022, uses the same description: seats that rotate, tip, and bounce, and no wheels. That page is [Baby Walkers: A Dangerous Choice](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-home/Pages/baby-walkers-a-dangerous-choice.aspx).

A seat-in baby walker is the object to throw out. HealthyChildren.org says walkers send children to the hospital, most often by rolling down stairs, and that a child in a walker can also be burned, drowned, or poisoned because the walker moves them into reach of a hazard. Most of those injuries happen while an adult is watching, because a child in a walker can move more than 3 feet in 1 second. Walkers do not teach walking. The AAP says they can delay it, and the AAP has called for a ban on baby walkers with wheels. The CDC’s [1-year tips](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html), reviewed 16 May 2026, also say baby walkers are not recommended. The CDC’s alternative on that page is holding your hands, holding safe furniture, or pushing a box, a chair, or a push toy. Taking the wheels off a walker does not turn it into a stationary activity center. [Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/consumer-product-safety/reports-publications/consumer-education/your-child-safe/play-time.html) says walkers are prohibited in Canada and should be destroyed.

| Object | What it is | What it is not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seated stationary center | A seat that surrounds the torso so the baby can bounce, spin, or step in place | A walker, a bed, or a play gym |
| Activity table | A low table the baby stands at and plays on | A surrounded seat, unless a seat is attached and the torso is enclosed |
| Activity cube | A toy with activities on the sides, used on the floor beside the baby | A seat, a walker, or a container to put the baby in |
| Play mat or play gym | A floor surface, sometimes with an arch, for a baby who is lying down | Covered in the play mat guide, not here |
| Play yard | An enclosed floor space where a baby can sit, crawl, or pull up | A seat. Sleep rules for a play yard are on the play mat page |

An activity table or cube is a toy the baby uses with their body free. It becomes the CPSC product only if a seat surrounds the torso. Read the label. If the label says stationary activity center, the checks below for that standard apply.

## When a baby can use one

There is no single birthday that makes a center safe. Three sources name different parts of the decision, and the label on the product is the fourth.

CPSC’s intended user is a child who can hold their head up unassisted and who is not yet able to walk. The [2019 CPSC rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2019/06/18/2019-12804/safety-standard-for-stationary-activity-centers) says the instructions that come with these products include developmental criteria for when to begin and when to stop. In that same rule, CPSC described the products then on the market as typically fitting a child under 25 pounds and 32 inches tall. That was a description of the market, not a limit you can apply to every model. Use the weight and height printed on yours.

A pediatrician writing for [UT Health San Antonio](https://news.uthscsa.edu/when-should-babies-take-their-first-independent-steps/) on 5 April 2023 says stationary bouncers, exercise saucers, and stationary activity chairs can be used for short, supervised periods once a baby has good head and trunk control, generally at 6 months or older, if the product is in good condition. The same article says they are not safe for sleep or for play without an adult. It also says the baby’s feet should reach the floor flat. That feet-flat note fits an open-base center, the type that is built so the feet can touch. A closed-base center is built so the feet do not touch in the positions the maker recommends. Do not add a book or a board to change that.

The CDC [Learn the Signs. Act Early.](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html) checklists say what most children, 75% or more, do by a given age. They are not a shopping list, and each age page says they are not a substitute for a validated screening tool. By [6 months](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/6-months.html), most babies lean on their hands when sitting. They are not yet sitting alone. By [9 months](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/9-months.html), most get to sitting and sit without support. By [12 months](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html), most pull up to stand and walk while holding furniture. The AAP discusses a stationary center in the 8-to-12-month movement chapter, and says some parents use one “at this stage, or even earlier.” That is not an instruction to start at 8 months, and it is not permission to start before the baby can hold their head up.

If your baby is not doing a skill you expected, or has lost a skill, the CDC says to talk with your child’s doctor and ask about developmental screening. Tell the doctor if your baby was born prematurely. This page is not that check.

## How long, and why the floor still matters

Stay where you can see them for the whole stay. The warning labels required for these products tell caregivers that a baby can fall and can be strangled, and the [2019 rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2019/06/18/2019-12804/safety-standard-for-stationary-activity-centers) says those labels and the instructions are how the hazard is explained. The example warnings in the current standard, described in CPSC’s [2025 update](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/03/27/2025-05239/safety-standard-for-stationary-activity-centers), include never leaving the child unattended, always keeping the child in view, and never using the product near stairs.

The AAP does not publish a minute limit for a stationary activity center. Two published limits still apply to the day around it.

The World Health Organization’s [2019 guidelines](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more) say an infant should not be restrained for more than 1 hour at a time. The examples are a pram or stroller, a high chair, or being strapped on a caregiver’s back. The guideline does not name activity centers. A seated center still holds a baby in one spot, so treat that hour as the longest single stay, and keep ordinary stays shorter. The UT Health article’s phrase is “short periods of time under supervision.” The same article says prolonged time in these seats, and in other seating devices, may cause temporary delays in motor development, or a temporary change in gait, such as more toe walking in the case of bouncers.

Floor play is the practice these seats do not provide. For an infant who is not mobile yet, WHO says at least 30 minutes in a prone position, tummy time, spread through the day while awake, as part of interactive floor play, and that more is better. The CDC [6-month](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/6-months.html) and [9-month](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/9-months.html) pages both say that playing on the floor or a play mat every day helps a baby move, learn, and explore. The sessions, the mat, and the arch are already explained in the play mat guide. A center is not tummy time, and it is not a substitute for it.

If your baby falls asleep in the seat, it is not a sleep product. UT Health says these centers are not safe for sleep. Move them to a firm, flat crib, bassinet, or play yard on the back, which is the sleep surface the AAP describes on [How to Keep Your Sleeping Baby Safe](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/sleep/Pages/a-parents-guide-to-safe-sleep.aspx), updated 11 September 2026.

Screens are a separate limit. The CDC milestone pages say screen time is not recommended under age 2, except a video call with someone the baby knows. If the tray includes a screen, leave that feature off. Lights and sounds are not required for the play. The American Academy of Pediatrics’ 2019 report in *Pediatrics*, [Selecting Appropriate Toys for Young Children in the Digital Era](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/143/1/e20183348/37330), says a useful toy is matched to the child’s skills and that the strongest play is the play a caregiver joins. How the rest of the day fits with screens is in <Link href="/blog/balancing-screen-time">balancing screen time</Link>.

## Safety checks

In the United States, a stationary activity center is a durable infant product. The mandatory standard is 16 CFR part 1238. As of 5 July 2025, that rule incorporates ASTM F2012-24. The standard covers sharp points, small parts, openings, pinching, stability, leg openings, and the toys sold with the product, among other hazards. CPSC’s page lists those requirements. Meeting the standard does not choose the right height or the right day. Use the checks below either way.

- **Flat floor, stable base.** Put it on a flat, level floor, which is what the stair-and-fall warning is paired with. The standard tests stability, including tipping. If the center leans, lifts, or tips while your baby uses it, stop.
- **Height adjustment.** Set the height the instructions describe. On an open-base center, use the feet-flat guide above. On a closed-base center, leave the feet off the floor the way the maker designed. CPSC’s 2019 incident review included height-adjustment mechanisms that failed. If the lock slips, stop using it.
- **Stairs, water, and heat.** Never use it near stairs. That line is in the standard’s warning examples. [Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/consumer-product-safety/reports-publications/consumer-education/your-child-safe/play-time.html) also says to keep an activity centre away from doors, windows, coffee tables, lamps, and stoves, fireplaces, or heaters, and away from curtain and blind cords. Keep it out of a bathroom and away from a pool, bucket, or any standing water. A walker can roll a baby into water. A stationary center does not roll, and a baby can still be beside water you cannot reach in time.
- **Strings.** Do not put a cord around the baby’s neck, and do not hang strings over the seat. The 2019 rule says the labels warn about strangulation as well as falls.
- **Small parts and loose toys.** Toys on the tray have to meet the toy standard. Anything a baby can pull off that fits entirely, without squeezing, inside the [small-parts cylinder](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/Small-Parts-for-Toys-and-Childrens-Products) is a choking hazard: 1.25 inches across and 2.25 inches deep. Health Canada says to check that the toys are not broken or loose before you put the baby in. Sharp points cut. The rest of that check is in the infant toy guide.
- **Straps, springs, and the seat opening.** Stop if a strap is torn, frayed, twisted, or detached, or if a spring has failed. Those are hazard patterns CPSC described in the 2019 rule. Also stop if the leg opening looks large enough for the baby to slip through. Leg-opening size is part of the standard because entrapment is one of the hazards it addresses.
- **Recalls and secondhand units.** Check [CPSC.gov/Recalls](https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls) before you use a new or used center, and register the product. CPSC requires a registration form on durable infant products so a recall can reach you. Health Canada’s advice for a used activity centre is the same idea: if it is damaged, treat it as unsafe, and check that it has not been recalled. The weight on the label has to cover your baby.

## How to play together

The useful part of the stay is you, not the tray. Sit where your baby can see your face. The CDC [6-month tips](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/6-months.html) say to play back and forth: when they smile, you smile, and when they make a sound, you copy it. Name one thing they hit. The [9-month tips](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/9-months.html) use that kind of label, such as “red, round ball.” Pass one toy back and forth if the piece is attached or too big for the cylinder. That is the CDC’s “my turn, your turn.”

Stop when the fun stops. The 6-month page says that if the baby is upset, take a break and comfort them. Then do the floor part. Put one toy just out of reach on the floor or a play mat so rolling, scooting, or crawling has a reason. That is a CDC 6-month and 9-month suggestion, and the setup for it is the play mat page. The center can follow a floor session. It should not replace one.

At a table or a cube, the same rule holds. You name one action, then you let them repeat it. You do not need every panel on at once.

## When to stop

Stop the seated center when any of these is true:

- Your baby can walk. CPSC’s intended user is a child who is not yet able to walk. The CDC’s walking practice at 12 months is holding your hands, holding safe furniture, or pushing a toy, which is the 1-year guide, not more time in a seat.
- Your baby reaches the weight or height on the label.
- Your baby is trying to climb out. The required warning is that a baby can fall from the product.
- A strap, spring, lock, or toy has failed, or the product is on a recall list.
- You are using the seat because the floor time did not happen.

An activity table can outlast the seat. The CDC 9-month tips say to put a baby near things they can pull up on safely, and the AAP says anything they cruise along needs to be weighted or fixed so it will not tip, with no sharp edges. Retire the table when they lean it over, when pieces come off, or when they have moved on to walking across the room. A push toy they walk behind is the next movement object, in the toddler gross-motor guide.

## Activity center ideas by type

These are types, not products. No brand, price, or rating belongs in this list.

| Type | What the baby does | Safety note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seated stationary center | Bounces, spins, or steps in place while you sit in view and name one toy | No wheels. Flat floor. Feet flat if the base is open. Never near stairs or water. Stop when they walk |
| Activity table | Stands and bats at a few large pieces, once they are pulling up | Must not tip when they lean. Not a surrounded seat unless the label says it is. Pieces stay bigger than the cylinder |
| Activity cube | Sits or crawls beside it and turns, slides, or hits one panel | On the floor, not up on a couch. No loose small parts. A battery door that opens with a coin is finished |
| DIY floor setup | Lies, rolls, or sits on a clear floor with one or two large toys and you beside them | The play mat page has the tummy-time sessions. Awake only. Move a sleepy baby to a firm crib, bassinet, or play yard |

The floor row is the one that does not require a purchase. Use a center type only after the checks above, and only for a short stay you are present for.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a baby activity center?

It is a stationary place for a baby who can hold their head up and cannot walk yet. The product CPSC regulates under that name surrounds the torso so the baby can rock, spin, bounce, or step in a limited range. An activity table and an activity cube are separate toys the baby uses while free to move. A walker with wheels is not an activity center.

### How is a stationary activity center different from a play gym?

A play gym is a mat on the floor with an arch of toys for a baby who is lying down and learning to look, bat, and kick. A stationary activity center holds the baby in a seat. The arch and the mat are in the play mat guide. The seat is this page.

### How is it different from a play yard?

A play yard is an enclosed floor. The AAP lists a play yard or playpen as a place for a baby who is learning to sit, crawl, or walk, in the same article that says to throw out wheeled walkers. A stationary activity center is a seat, not that enclosure. Sleeping belongs on the back in a crib, bassinet, or play yard that meets the sleep rules on the play mat page, not in the activity center.

### When can a baby use an activity center?

When they can hold their head up unassisted, they are inside the weight and height on the label, and they are not walking yet. A pediatric source puts good head and trunk control at about 6 months or older for a short, supervised stay. The CDC’s “sits without support” line is a 9-month milestone for most babies, not the start date printed on the product. Read the label.

### How long can a baby stay in an activity center?

The AAP does not give a minute count. Keep it short, and stay where you can see them. WHO’s limit for an infant held in one place, such as a high chair or stroller, is 1 hour at a time, and that hour is a ceiling rather than a goal. Floor play, including tummy time for a baby who is not mobile yet, is still the main part of the day.

### Are seat-in baby walkers a kind of activity center?

No. Walkers have wheels. The AAP says they do not teach walking, can delay walking, and can move a baby down stairs or into a burn, a poison, or water faster than you can stop them. The CDC says they are not recommended. A stationary center has no wheels. Removing the wheels from a walker does not make it one.

### When should we stop using it?

Stop the seated center when your baby can walk, when they hit the printed weight or height, when they try to climb out, or when any part fails or the product is recalled. Keep the floor. The 1-year page is the next toy list.

## What to try first

Clear a patch of floor and get down on it for one awake session. The play mat page has the length of that session. If you already have a stationary center, use it afterward, on a flat floor away from stairs and water, for a short stay you watch the whole time. Name one thing your baby touches. Then take them out.

If they cannot hold their head up, leave the seat empty. If they are already walking, leave it empty too, and use the <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-1-year-olds">age-1 toy guide</Link> instead.
