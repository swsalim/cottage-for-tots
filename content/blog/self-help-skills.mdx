A useful educational toy for an autistic child practices one daily living skill the child is already close to doing. That might be pulling a large zipper, putting a cup on the table, or moving a picture when a step is finished. A toy that only flashes and talks is a poor match for that job.

This page is about self-help and daily living: dressing, washing, eating, tidying, and short routines. It is not medical advice, and a toy is not a therapy plan. The [CDC’s treatment overview](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/treatment/index.html) says supports can happen at home, in school, in health care, and in the community. If dressing, feeding, toileting, or safety is the real goal, work with the child’s doctor, occupational therapist, speech therapist, or teacher.

For how to choose toys in general, see the <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-child-development">guide to educational toys</Link>. Household jobs that are not written for autism, such as pouring and wiping, are in <Link href="/blog/montessori-activities-2-year-olds">Montessori-inspired activities for 2-year-olds</Link>.

## What daily living skills are

Daily living skills are the ordinary jobs of looking after yourself and taking part in a routine. The CDC says occupational therapy teaches skills that help a person live as independently as possible, and it names dressing, eating, bathing, and relating to people as examples. The American Academy of Pediatrics, on [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/conditions/developmental-disabilities/Pages/occupational-therapy.aspx), describes the same kinds of jobs: getting dressed, using a spoon, and brushing teeth. An occupational therapist can look at motor planning and sensory processing and then suggest what to practice at home. The goals still belong to that child.

A toy can give a low-pressure copy of one of those actions. It does not teach the whole routine, and it does not replace the real coat, the real cup, or a therapist’s plan.

The CDC’s [signs and symptoms page](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/signs-symptoms/index.html) describes autism spectrum disorder as a developmental disability that can affect social communication and interaction, and that can include restricted or repetitive behaviors or interests. Some children also react strongly to how things sound, smell, taste, look, or feel, or need a routine to stay the same. The CDC notes that a child may not show every example on that list. Toy choice starts from what this child does, not from a stereotype.

## Pick one skill, then one toy

The [AAP’s toy-buying tips for children with special needs](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/Toy-Buying-Tips-for-Children-with-Special-Needs.aspx) say to choose for developmental age, not the birthday on the box, and to pick toys you can use together. The same page says expensive electronic toys are unnecessary, and that adults and children talk less when play is electronic. A dressing board, a doll with large fasteners, a play cup, or a picture strip is enough.

The AAP also says to ask the child’s therapist for ideas, and that a toy can be adjusted with foam, Velcro, or larger buttons when grip or vision makes the standard piece hard to use.

Match the toy to one action:

| Daily job | Toy type to look for | Keep it to one action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dressing | Dressing board, doll clothes, a large zipper, button, or snap | One fastener. Then the same movement on a real loose garment when the child is ready. |
| Washing and teeth | Toy mirror, pretend brush, washcloth | The pretend set only. Real toothpaste, mouthwash, and cleaners stay with you. |
| Eating and kitchen play | Play cup, large spoon, pot, basket | Place, stir, or put away. No heat, no real knives, no tiny lids. |
| Tidying | One basket and one kind of object | The object goes to one labeled spot. Stop while the basket is still easy. |
| A short routine | Photos or drawings of two or three steps | The child can see the next step. You still model it. |

Hand skills for buttons and lids, when the child is a toddler and you are using household objects, are in <Link href="/blog/fine-motor-activities-toddlers">fine motor activities for toddlers</Link>. Preschool fastening toys are in <Link href="/blog/fine-motor-toys-preschoolers">fine motor toys for preschoolers</Link>.

## Dressing, washing, and household play

**Dressing.** Start with the fastener the child can already touch without distress. A large zipper on a stable board, or one big button on a doll’s shirt, is a first step. The AAP’s suggestion to add a larger button or Velcro is a fit change, not a new skill. When that movement is comfortable, try the same words and the same order on a loose piece of the child’s own clothing. Ages for dressing skills are on the [CDC milestone checklists](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html), collected in <Link href="/blog/gross-fine-motor-skills">gross versus fine motor skills</Link>. They are not a test you give at home.

**Washing and teeth.** A toy mirror and a pretend brush let you show “pick up, brush, put back” without putting product in a mouth. Keep the real toothbrush routine as its own supervised job. If the child will not touch the brush, a therapist can help you decide whether the issue is grip, taste, sound, or the number of steps.

**Kitchen and tidying.** A play kitchen is useful when it stands for one household action: cup on the table, spoon in the pot, cloth on the spill, toy back in the basket. The CDC’s description of the TEACCH approach says daily routines can be written or drawn and put where the child can see them, and that spoken instructions can be paired with a picture or a demonstration. Two to four pictures are enough. If the child ignores the drawings, try a photo of the actual cup, or fewer steps.

Food, water, and heat are not toy practice. Stay for those. Pieces that come off should be too large to choke on. The CPSC says to [keep small balls and toys with small parts away from children younger than 3](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Toys), and to follow the age label.

## Use a picture for the next step

Some children speak. Some use a gesture, a sign, a picture, or a device. The CDC lists all of those as ways people communicate, in its note on speech and language therapy. A toy does not require eye contact or a scripted sentence to “count.”

A simple sequence sits beside the toy:

1. Point to the first picture, or hand the child the object.
2. Model the action once. Use the same short words each time.
3. Wait. Accept a reach, a point, a sign, a sound, or a word.
4. Move that picture aside when the step is done.

The CDC describes one behavioral teaching style, discrete trial training, as breaking a lesson into small parts. You do not need a clinic setup to borrow that idea: open the container today, and add “put the cup away” on another day. The CDC also describes Floor Time (DIR) as following the child’s interests to open more chances to communicate. If the child lines up cars, the daily-living step might be “car goes in the box,” not a new toy that interrupts the line.

Stop when the child is distressed. A different picture, a shorter job, or a different texture is a better next try than repeating the same demand.

## Safety and fit

Check the child in front of you, then the label.

- Follow the age guidance on the package. The CPSC’s [toy safety tips](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Toys) say to choose toys that match the child’s interests and abilities, and to throw packaging away so it does not become a plaything.
- The AAP says children with special needs can face extra injury risk when their development does not match the age on the package, citing a 2016 study, and to avoid small parts, small balls, marbles, and balloons if the child is small for their age or has trouble swallowing. CPSC also says to keep deflated balloons away from children younger than 8.
- If a toy uses batteries, CPSC [toy safety guidance](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Toy-Safety) says battery compartments have to keep children from reaching the battery during play. A loose coin battery is not a piece of a dressing toy.
- Loose magnets are a swallowing hazard. CPSC [toy safety guidance](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Toy-Safety) sets limits on magnets that can come loose. A zipper or a cup does not need a magnetic toy.
- Supervise a new toy until you know whether the child mouths it, throws it, or climbs on it.
- Toileting, feeding safety, falls, and mobility are not things to solve with a catalog. Ask the child’s clinician.

The wider hazard list for infants, including what to inspect on a used toy, is in <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-infants">safe toys for infants</Link>. The same checks still matter for toddlers.

## Frequently asked questions

### How can toys practice daily living skills for autistic children?

They give one action a clear, repeatable form. A dressing board lets a child pull one zipper before trying a coat. A picture of a cup next to a play cup shows where the cup goes. The CDC says occupational therapy teaches skills such as dressing, eating, and bathing. The AAP says those strategies are then practiced at home and at school. The toy is the practice object. The therapist, teacher, or caregiver is the person who decides the goal.

### Which toys support dressing, hygiene, and self-care?

Look for one movement at a time: a large zipper, a large button, doll clothes, a toy mirror, or a pretend brush. The AAP suggests adjusting the piece with a larger button, Velcro, or foam if the child cannot grip the standard one. Check texture and noise before you commit. Real toothpaste, medicines, and cleaning products are not part of the toy.

### What toys help with cooking, cleaning, and household routines?

A play cup, a pot, a cloth, a basket, and a few pictures can stand in for “set one place” or “put it away.” Keep the set small. Connect it to a real cup or a real wipe only when you are there to supervise food, water, and heat. Ordinary home versions of pouring and wiping are in the <Link href="/blog/montessori-activities-2-year-olds">Montessori activities</Link> post.

### How do visual supports make these toys more useful?

The child can see the step instead of holding a spoken list in mind. The CDC’s description of TEACCH says routines can be written or drawn and placed in sight, and that words can be paired with a picture or a demonstration. Use the fewest pictures that still show the order. If drawings fail, try a photo of the real object.

### How do I choose a toy without creating frustration?

Use the AAP checks: developmental age, a toy you can play with together, and a therapist’s suggestion when you have one. The child should be able to do the first step with a little help. Change one thing if it falls apart: fewer pieces, a larger handle, or a shorter sequence. Distress is a reason to stop, not a reason to add another toy.

### How do I use a toy during a real routine?

Keep the words and the pictures the same. Practice the play cup, then later use the real cup at snack with you beside the child. One new step is enough. The CDC’s note on early intervention says services from birth to age 3 can help a child with skills such as talking, walking, and interacting, and that a parent can ask for an evaluation without waiting on a referral. A toy does not take the place of that referral. Details are on the CDC’s [accessing services](https://www.cdc.gov/autism/treatment/accessing-services.html) page.

### Do educational toys replace speech therapy or occupational therapy?

No. The CDC lists speech therapy, occupational therapy, and educational supports as treatments. Toys can sit inside a plan those professionals help you make. They are not the plan. The AAP says to ask a speech, occupational, or physical therapist which play is worth trying at home.

### When should I talk with a doctor instead of buying another toy?

Talk with the child’s doctor if you are worried about development, if a skill has dropped away, or if feeding, toileting, falls, or distress during care is the problem. The CDC’s [milestone guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html) says not to wait, and that the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends developmental screening at 9, 18, and 30 months and autism screening at 18 and 24 months, or whenever there is a concern. This article is not that screening.

## One place to start

Name one action, such as “zipper” or “cup on the table.” Pick one toy that shows that action, and one picture if pictures help. Play it together, then stop. If the same action shows up later in the real routine with less help, keep going. If it does not, change the toy or ask the child’s therapist before you buy a second one.
