A sensory toy for a toddler is any toy whose main job is something to feel, hear, watch, pour, or move with. A textured ball, a pair of shakers, a scoop and a funnel, a fabric tunnel. Choose one by the sense your child keeps going back to, then check the piece size. A toy can invite exploration. It is not a therapy, and it does not guarantee a milestone.

## What sensory play looks like at this age

Toddlers learn what things do by handling them. The CDC [Learn the Signs. Act Early.](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html) checklists are communication tools, not a screening test. The [key points](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/key-points.html) page says the milestones were placed at ages when about 75% or more of children would be expected to show them, and that the checklists should not be used to screen for or diagnose a delay. Each age page says the materials are not a substitute for a validated screening tool.

By 18 months, most children walk without holding on, scribble, climb on and off a couch or chair without help, and play with toys in a simple way, such as pushing a toy car. Those lines are on the CDC [18-month checklist](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html), reviewed 16 May 2026. By 2 years, most children try switches, knobs, or buttons on a toy, hold something in one hand while the other hand works (the example is holding a container and taking the lid off), kick a ball, and run. Those lines are on the CDC [2-year checklist](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html), reviewed 16 May 2026.

The CDC tips for this age read like a sensory shelf. For 18-month-olds: roll balls back and forth, put blocks or other items in and out of containers, and blow bubbles for your child to pop. For 2-year-olds: sand toys, plastic containers, spoons, or a funnel in the tub or sandbox, and finger paint on paper. NAEYC’s [toys by age](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/topics/play/toys) adds objects to sort by size, shape, color, and smell, sand and water play toys, and rhythm instruments for 2-year-olds. The same page says each child develops at their own pace, and that a safe toy from another age list can still fit.

## Sensory toys by sense

| Sense | What you see them do | Look for | Leave behind |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Touch | Squeeze, rub, poke, press | Textured balls, fabric squares, play dough you can supervise | Water beads, loose stuffing, tiny squishes |
| Sound | Shake, bang, tap | Shakers sealed shut, a drum, bells sewn or fixed in | Shakers that unscrew, toys with a button battery |
| Sight | Watch, chase, peek | Bubbles, a sealed bottle, an unbreakable mirror | Toys that flash and play on their own |
| Water and sand | Pour, scoop, fill | Cups, a funnel, a scoop, a sieve | Rice and beans for a child who still mouths, glass jars |
| Movement and body | Roll, crawl through, rock | A soft ball, a fabric tunnel, a low cushion | A ball small enough to pass the 1.75-inch hole, balloons |

## Touch: textures, squeeze, and dough

The useful touch toy has more than one surface and stays in one piece. A ball with bumps. A set of fabric squares, each a different weave. A sturdy board book with a patch to rub. NAEYC lists textured balls and squeeze toys for young infants, from birth through 6 months. Its own note is that a safe toy from another age list can still be a good choice. A 2-year-old who still wants that ball is using a toy the list already allows.

Play dough is a good touch material for a child who no longer eats everything. Store-bought dough is the simpler choice. Homemade dough and raw flour are covered in <Link href="/blog/sensory-play-activities-toddlers">sensory play activities for toddlers</Link>. Stay at the table. Put it away while a younger sibling is awake.

**Look for:** seams that hold when you tug, textures you can wipe clean, and pieces too big for the small-parts cylinder described below.

**Skip:** water beads in any form. The [CPSC water-bead safety alert](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/SafetyAlert_5101_WaterBeads_082024.pdf) says the way to reduce the hazard is to remove them from any environment where children 3 and younger may live or visit. Also skip a gel or bead-filled squeeze toy, and any squish small enough to bite through. Age, tears, and cleaning for that object are in <Link href="/blog/squishy-toys">squishy toys for kids</Link>.

## Sound: shakers, drums, and bells

A sound toy should make a sound because your child did something. Shake, tap, bang. The CDC 2-year checklist includes trying switches, knobs, or buttons on a toy. The 2-year tips suggest a toy where the child pushes a button and something happens. A drum, a pair of shakers, or a pot and a wooden spoon covers the same idea without a speaker. How rhythm and music fit into play is in <Link href="/blog/musical-milestones-music-toys">music toys and child development</Link>.

A toy that plays a whole song when touched once does the work for them. In a study of 26 parent-infant pairs, with infants 10 to 16 months old, play with electronic toys came with fewer adult words and fewer conversational turns than play with books or traditional toys ([Sosa, JAMA Pediatrics, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26720437/)). That sample is infants, not every toddler. It is a reason to prefer a toy that waits for a shake, a tap, or a bang, so you still have something to say.

**Look for:** shakers sealed so the filling cannot come out, bells fixed inside or stitched on, and a drum with a mallet too large for the small-parts cylinder. Federal rules also ban a toy whose noisemaking parts can be dislodged or pulled off and then inhaled or swallowed ([16 CFR 1500.18(a)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-II/subchapter-C/part-1500/section-1500.18)).

**Skip:** anything with a battery compartment your child can open. HealthyChildren.org, from the American Academy of Pediatrics, says to [avoid toys with button batteries or high-powered magnets](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/pages/What-to-Look-for-in-a-Toy.aspx).

## Sight: bubbles, bottles, and mirrors

Watching is sensory play too. Bubbles are on the CDC 18-month tip list. A sealed bottle with one large object inside gives a toddler something to turn and follow. How to tape that cap, and which fills to leave out, is the <Link href="/blog/diy-sensory-bottles-toddlers">DIY sensory bottles</Link> page. An unbreakable mirror is on NAEYC’s list for young infants. The same page says safe toys should be shatter-proof.

Light-up toys are a judgment call. A single light that comes on when a child presses a button is the cause-and-effect the CDC 2-year tips describe. A toy that flashes and plays on its own gives the child less to do. The AAP clinical report [Selecting Appropriate Toys for Young Children in the Digital Era](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30509931/) (Healey and Mendelsohn, Pediatrics, 2019) is written for pediatricians to use with families. It notes the lack of evidence that digital “toys” bring the same developmental benefits as traditional toys, and it promotes toys used together with a caregiver.

**Look for:** a bottle with the cap taped so it will not turn, a mirror labeled unbreakable or shatterproof, and bubbles you blow while the child chases.

**Skip:** glitter, beads, and a cap your child can twist. Those fills and the seal test are in the bottles post.

## Water and sand: pour, scoop, fill

Pouring and filling are the experiment the CDC already names at 2 years: plastic containers, spoons, or a funnel in the tub or sandbox. A scoop, a sieve, and two cups of different sizes are a full kit. Sink or float, with objects you already have, is in <Link href="/blog/sensory-play-activities-toddlers">sensory play activities for toddlers</Link>. Choosing, rinsing, and drying the toys that stay in the bath is in <Link href="/blog/bath-toys">bath toys for babies and toddlers</Link>.

Water play needs an adult within reach, every time, including a shallow tray. On [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/at-play/Pages/Water-Safety-And-Young-Children.aspx), updated 18 May 2026, the American Academy of Pediatrics says young children can drown in as little as an inch or two of water, quickly and silently, and that an adult should stay within arm’s length and use touch supervision at bath time and swim time.

**Look for:** cups and funnels too big for the cylinder, a sieve with no sharp edge, and a tray with a lip so a spill stays on the tray.

**Skip:** dry rice or beans as the “sand” for a child who still mouths. Bin fillers to leave out are listed in the sensory activities post. Glass jars stay out. Small pieces in the water get the same cylinder test as any other toy.

## Movement and body: balls, tunnels, and rocking

Movement is a sense too. Rolling a ball, crawling through a tunnel, and rocking all give the body information. NAEYC lists tunnels, and low climbers with soft material underneath, among large-muscle toys for 2-year-olds. Rainy-day games that use the same body are in <Link href="/blog/indoor-gross-motor-activities-toddlers">indoor gross motor activities</Link>. Tunnel length, balls, and low climbers as a purchase are in <Link href="/blog/gross-motor-toys-toddlers">gross motor toys for toddlers</Link>.

The AAP 2012 policy statement on [sensory integration therapies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22641765/) is about therapy, not a shopping list. It describes sensory-based therapies as activities believed to organize the sensory system by providing vestibular (balance and movement), proprioceptive (body position), auditory, and tactile input, using equipment such as brushes, swings, and balls. A soft ball or a fabric tunnel at home is ordinary play. It is not that therapy.

**Look for:** a soft ball you can see is much larger than 1.75 inches across, a fabric tunnel with the frame covered, and a low firm cushion on a clear floor, with soft material under anything they climb.

**Skip:** balloons. HealthyChildren.org’s [choking prevention](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/injuries-emergencies/Pages/Choking-Prevention.aspx) page says balloons, balls, marbles, pieces of toys, and small round foods cause the most choking deaths, and that balloons are a choking risk through age 8. Also skip any ball small enough to pass the test below.

## Piece size, because toddlers still mouth toys

Children’s products intended for children under 3 that present a choking, aspiration, or ingestion hazard because of small parts are banned. A small part is any object that fits entirely, without compressing and in any orientation, into the [small-parts cylinder](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/Small-Parts-16-C-F-R-Part-1501-English.pdf): 2.25 inches long by 1.25 inches wide (57.10 mm by 31.70 mm). The rule is [16 CFR part 1501](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/Small-Parts-for-Toys-and-Childrens-Products). A ball intended for children under 3 is banned if, under its own weight, it passes entirely through a 1.75-inch (44.4 mm) hole in any orientation ([16 CFR 1500.18(a)(17)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-II/subchapter-C/part-1500/section-1500.18)).

Sensory kits sold online sometimes bundle small fidgets, beads, or slime with toddler toys. Read the age label and check each piece yourself. The ban applies to children’s products intended for children under 3. A kitchen cup or a wooden spoon is not sold as that product, so the cylinder is still the check before it goes in a mouth. The wider list, including cords, magnets, recalls, and hand-me-downs, is in <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-for-toddlers">safe toys for toddlers</Link>. Clean fabric and wooden pieces between uses. The method is in <Link href="/blog/clean-wooden-toys">how to clean wooden toys</Link>.

## Sensory toys and autism or sensory differences

Many parents search for sensory toys for autistic toddlers. Toys can be part of play for any child. They are not a treatment. The same AAP 2012 statement says difficulty tolerating or processing sensory information can be seen in many conditions, including autism spectrum disorder and ADHD, that other developmental and behavioral disorders must be considered, and that sensory processing disorder generally should not be diagnosed. It says research on sensory integration therapy is limited and inconclusive, and that occupational therapy using sensory-based activities may be one part of a broader plan.

If your child is very upset by noise or texture, or seeks strong movement in a way that worries you, talk with your child’s doctor. The CDC milestone pages say not to wait if a child is not meeting milestones, has lost skills, or you have other concerns. Toys for daily routines, chosen with a therapist, are in <Link href="/blog/self-help-skills">educational toys for autistic children</Link>.

## Sensory toy ideas by type

These are types, not products. No brand, price, or rating belongs in this list. Use the piece-size check above before any of them reach the floor.

| Toy type | Sense it invites | Safety note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Textured ball set | Touch, movement | Each ball bigger than the 1.75-inch hole |
| Fabric or texture squares | Touch | Stitching holds when tugged |
| Sealed shakers or egg shakers | Sound | Cannot be opened; too big for the cylinder |
| Toddler drum | Sound | Mallet too big for the cylinder |
| Bubble wand and solution | Sight | Adult holds the solution |
| Sealed sensory bottle | Sight | Cap taped so it will not turn |
| Unbreakable mirror | Sight | Labeled unbreakable or shatterproof, no glass |
| Tub tray or water tray with cups and a funnel | Water, touch | Adult within arm’s reach the whole time |
| Sand scoop, sieve, and bucket | Touch, pour | No sharp edges; empty standing water |
| Fabric play tunnel | Movement | Frame covered; you can see them inside |
| Store-bought play dough with large tools | Touch | Adult at the table |

A kitchen cup can stand in for the scoop. A pot can stand in for the drum. Buy when that type is missing, broken, or too small.

## How many sensory toys a toddler needs

There is no official number. In a study of 36 toddlers, children given 4 toys played for longer stretches and in more ways than when given 16 ([Dauch and colleagues, Infant Behavior and Development, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29190457/)). The paper does not set a number for your shelf. For one sensory session, put out one material so you can see which sense they return to. How to store the rest and swap a few is in <Link href="/blog/toy-rotation">how to set up a toy rotation</Link>. Calm, low-noise toys for focus are in <Link href="/blog/toys-improve-focus-concentration">toys for concentration</Link>.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are sensory toys for toddlers?

Toys whose main job is something to feel, hear, see, pour, or move with. Textured balls, shakers, bubbles, scoops, and tunnels all count. They do not need to be labeled sensory.

### What sensory toys are safe for a 1-year-old?

Ones with no piece that fits in the small-parts cylinder, no button battery, no high-powered magnets, and no water beads. Soft textured balls, sealed shakers, board books with textures, and stacking cups are a start. More for this age, sorted by skill, is in <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-1-year-olds">educational toys for 1-year-olds</Link>. The hazard checklist is in <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-for-toddlers">safe toys for toddlers</Link>.

### Are sensory toys only for autistic children?

No. Every toddler explores through the senses. For a child with a diagnosis or sensory differences, choose with their therapist. Toys are not a treatment. The limits of that idea are in the section above.

## What to try first

Watch one play session. Note which sense your child keeps returning to: squeezing, banging, pouring, or rolling. Pull one item for that sense from the kitchen or the toy box. If nothing fits, use the table above to pick one type, and check it against the cylinder before it reaches the floor.
