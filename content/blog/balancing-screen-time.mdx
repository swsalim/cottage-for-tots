The hard part of screen time is what it replaces. Sleep, play, reading, and a conversation all need a stretch of the day with the screen off. This page is how to protect those stretches for babies, toddlers, and preschoolers, and how to use a screen when it is on.

What happens during play once the screen is off is in <Link href="/blog/education-toys-help-kids-learn">how educational toys help children learn</Link>. A rainy-day movement plan is in <Link href="/blog/indoor-gross-motor-activities-toddlers">indoor gross motor activities</Link>. One book and one new word is in <Link href="/blog/reading-aloud-language-development">reading aloud</Link>.

**Quick answer:** Make a family media plan for where, when, and what. Watch with your child. Keep meals, bedrooms, and the hour before bed screen-free. If you want a number, the CDC tip sheets reviewed in May 2026 still say video calls only before age 2, and no more than 1 hour a day of a children's program with an adult from ages 2 to 4. The American Academy of Pediatrics' 2026 policy says that number is a starting point. The test is whether sleep, play, and time with you still happen.

## What the 2026 guidance changed

The AAP policy statement [Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/157/2/e2025075320/206129/) (*Pediatrics*, 2026) treats screens as one part of a larger digital world: television, apps, games, video chat, and tools that are built to keep people watching. The parent explanation on [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/helping-kids-thrive-in-a-digital-world-AAP-policy-explained.aspx), updated 3 June 2026, says the useful focus is quality, context, and conversation, not a single limit for every family.

That page is written by Tiffany Munzer, MD, FAAP, lead author of the policy. It says infants under 18 months learn best from real people. Heavy solo screen use can affect language and social skills. How many hours, and whether a screen is being used to calm or to entertain, both matter. Misuse in the early years is linked, on that page, with delays in language, thinking, social skills, and fine motor development, with poor sleep, with less time spent learning with the people who love them, and with more angry outbursts.

The same page is honest about why screens win on a hard day. Long work hours, thin childcare, and few safe places to play make a screen the easy option. A plan still helps. It does not require a perfect house.

## If you want a number

Two current sources still give families a number when they ask for one.

The CDC milestone tip sheets, reviewed in May 2026, say:

- Before age 2, limit screens to video calls with people the child knows. The [6-month](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/6-months.html) and [1-year](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html) pages say other screen time is not recommended. Babies learn by talking, playing, and being with people.
- At [2 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html), [3 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html), and [4 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html), no more than 1 hour a day of a children's program, with an adult there. No screens in the bedroom.
- At 4 years, avoid screens for 1 to 2 hours before bed. That page says children this age need 10 to 13 hours of sleep a day, including naps.

The AAP's own answers for clinicians line up with that hour for toddlers and preschoolers, and they add a small exception for infants. The [infant and toddler guidance](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/discussing-screen-time-with-parents/) says exposure besides video chat should stay at occasional, brief, high-quality video, such as a short Sesame Street clip, and that toddlers older than 18 months stay at no more than 1 hour a day when a family wants a limit. The policy statement says infants do not learn from digital media, and that an occasional brief, high-quality video is not harmful.

Use the hour as a ceiling for entertainment, not as a target to fill. School and video calls sit outside that entertainment hour. Background television still counts as a screen in the room. The infant guidance asks families to limit it even when the baby is not watching.

## Write the plan on one page

The AAP's [Family Media Plan](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/fmp/Pages/MediaPlan.aspx) is a free page you can fill in and print. The note on [cutting back with a toddler](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/reducing-screen-time-for-toddlers-cold-turkey-or-gradual-change/) says you do not have to go cold turkey. Answer three questions:

1. **Where.** One shared screen in a common room. Delay a personal tablet. Bedrooms stay off the list.
2. **When.** A predictable time, such as a weekend morning or a short block after rest, beats screens that appear whenever the day gets loud. Protect meals and the hour before bed for the whole family, including your phone.
3. **What.** One show or one video you have already watched. The 2026 policy points to high-quality, child-centered media that models feelings, includes a learning goal, and leaves room for play. It names PBS Kids, including Daniel Tiger's Neighborhood, and Sesame Workshop as examples. Skip autoplay, in-app rewards, and anything you have not seen.

Put the answers where you can see them. A child who knows the time is coming asks less often outside it. If the only device is a phone, the same three answers still work: one room, one time, one program.

## Watch it with them

Co-viewing is the part that turns a program into a conversation. HealthyChildren.org says watching and playing alongside your child is how you know what they are taking in. The CDC sheets say the adult is present for that hour.

Sit where you can see the picture. Name one thing: "Daniel is waiting for a turn." When the program ends, turn the screen off yourself. Autoplay is a design choice, not a reason to start the next episode. Then do one thing from the program with real objects, if they want to. A song you both dance to counts. A lecture about the lesson does not.

Video chat is different from a show. A grandparent on a call can respond, which a cartoon cannot. Keep the call short enough that the child is still in it. Hold them so they can see the face, and wave when it is time to stop.

## Fill the hour you took back

A limit fails when the replacement is an empty room and a parent who is still on a phone. Have one offline activity ready before you turn the screen off.

- **Move.** Fifteen minutes in a cleared room is in the indoor gross-motor guide. The CDC 4-year page suggests red light, green light or freeze dance as a way to practice stopping.
- **Read.** One book, with the child on your lap or beside you. The reading-aloud guide keeps it to one new word.
- **Hands.** Playdough, a few blocks, or paper and a crayon. Setup and what to skip are in <Link href="/blog/art-activities-preschoolers-household-items">art activities with household items</Link> and <Link href="/blog/sensory-play-activities-toddlers">sensory play</Link>.
- **One toy, then stop directing.** How to set a material out and leave it alone is in <Link href="/blog/creative-play-grow">creative play at home</Link>.

If the screen was covering a time you cannot play, say so and offer one of those, even for five minutes. Five minutes of a shared book beats an hour you resent and then give back to the tablet.

## Your phone is part of the plan

Children copy the screen they see in your hand. The 2-month CDC page asks caregivers to limit their own screen time while they are with the baby, so they notice the baby's signals. HealthyChildren.org says to put your phone down at meals and at bedtime, and to take breaks where the child can see you do it.

You do not have to quit your phone. You do have to pick the moments that belong to the child: feeding, the bath, the book, the first ten minutes after you walk in. A <Link href="/blog/bath-toys">bath</Link> needs you within arm's reach anyway. The phone can wait in another room.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much screen time is okay for a toddler or preschooler?

If you want a number, use no more than 1 hour a day of a children's program, with you there, for ages 2 to 4. That is what the CDC tip sheets say, and it matches the AAP's practical limit for toddlers and preschoolers. The 2026 AAP policy says the more important check is whether sleep, play, reading, and time with you are still intact.

### What about babies?

Keep screens to video calls. The CDC does not recommend other screen time under age 2. The AAP says an occasional brief, high-quality video is not harmful, and that infants learn from people, not from programs. Turn off a television that is only on in the background.

### Do we have to stop all screens at once?

No. The AAP's toddler guidance says a plan beats cold turkey for a child who already watches. Choose the room, the time, and the program. Shrink the time over a couple of weeks if a sudden stop makes the day fall apart. Young children do not need a screen in order to learn.

### Are educational apps worth it?

Some programs are built around children's development, and some are built to keep a child tapping. The AAP policy describes the better ones as modeling social and emotional skills, teaching toward a clear goal, and leaving room for play. Watch it yourself first. Then watch it with your child, and turn it off when the hour, or their interest, ends. A block in the hand still does a job an app cannot. That job is described in the guide to how toys help children learn.

### What if I need the screen so I can cook or work?

Use it on purpose, for a short, known show, in the common room. Say when it will end. Pair it with something afterward that you can do from the kitchen, such as a book they already know or a basket with one safe toy. The need is real. The plan is what keeps that need from becoming the whole day.

## What to try first

Write the three lines today: where the screen lives, when it turns on, and which one program is allowed. Put your phone in another room for the next meal. Have one book or one ball ready for the moment the program ends.
