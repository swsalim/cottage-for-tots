Choose a toy for a 1-year-old by the skill they are already practicing, not by the word “educational” on the box. One cup, one board book, one soft ball, and one doll cover more of this year than a pile of new toys. A toy can invite practice. It cannot guarantee a milestone.

This page is the age-1 shelf, about 12 to 24 months. The age-3 shelf is in <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-3-year-olds">educational toys for 3-year-olds</Link>. The wider buying test, from babies through school age, is in the <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-child-development">guide to choosing educational toys</Link>. Safety features for infants still under 1 are in <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-infants">safe toys for infants</Link>.

## What most 1-year-olds are practicing

The CDC [Learn the Signs. Act Early.](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/index.html) checklists describe what most children, 75% or more, do by a given age. They are not a shopping list or a screening test. The CDC says that on its [key points](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/key-points.html) page, and each age page below says the materials are not a substitute for a validated screening tool. The movement lines, sorted into big-body skills and hand skills, are already in <Link href="/blog/gross-fine-motor-skills">gross versus fine motor skills</Link>.

By 12 months, most babies play a game such as pat-a-cake, wave bye-bye, and call a parent “mama,” “dada,” or another special name. Most put a block in a cup, look for a toy you hide, pull up to stand, and walk while holding furniture. Most pick up a small bit of food between thumb and finger. Those lines are on the CDC [1-year checklist](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html), reviewed 16 May 2026.

By 15 months, most children take a few steps on their own, stack at least two small objects, try one or two words besides mama or dada, and hug a stuffed doll. By 18 months, most walk without holding on, scribble, try a spoon, look at a few pages with you, and play with a toy in a simple way, such as pushing a car. By 2 years, most kick a ball, run, say at least two words together, and play with more than one toy at once, such as putting toy food on a toy plate. Those lines are on the CDC checklists for [15 months](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/15-months.html), [18 months](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/18-months.html), and [2 years](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/2-years.html), each reviewed 16 May 2026.

A child who is 12 months and not walking yet is still inside that picture. Pulling up and cruising furniture are the 12-month movement lines. Walking alone is later. Match the toy to the child in front of you.

| Skill | What you watch them do | Look for | Leave behind |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hands | Pick up, drop in, stack two, scribble | A cup, a chunky crayon, large pieces | Beads, coins, anything they can swallow |
| Movement | Pull up, cruise, then walk and kick | A push toy, a soft ball much larger than 1.75 inches | A seat-in baby walker, balloons |
| First words and books | Wave, name, point, look at pages | A board book, a doll you can talk about | A toy that says every word for them |
| Pretend and cause and effect | Hug a doll, push a car, try a knob | One doll, one car, one big button | A set with a script and many tiny pieces |
| Sorting and stacking | Fill, dump, stack, try a shape | Cups, a few blocks, a sorter with large pieces | A 20-piece puzzle, loose magnets |

NAEYC’s notes on [toys for 1-year-olds](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/topics/play/toys) point to board books, blocks, and things a child can use in more than one way. That is the filter for every row. The American Academy of Pediatrics, on [HealthyChildren.org](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/pages/What-to-Look-for-in-a-Toy.aspx), says the best toys match the skills a child has now, and that no single toy is required for the next milestone.

## Piece size, because they still mouth toys

Children in this window still put toys in their mouths. Toys intended for children under 3 that present a choking hazard because of small parts are banned. A small part is any object that fits entirely, without compressing and in any orientation, into the [small-parts cylinder](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/Small-Parts-16-C-F-R-Part-1501-English.pdf): 2.25 inches long by 1.25 inches wide (57.10 mm by 31.70 mm). That includes a whole toy, a separate piece, and a piece that breaks off in use-and-abuse testing. The rule is [16 CFR part 1501](https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/Small-Parts-for-Toys-and-Childrens-Products).

A ball intended for children under 3 is banned if it passes, under its own weight, entirely through a circular hole 1.75 inches (44.4 mm) across. That rule is [16 CFR 1500.18(a)(17)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-II/subchapter-C/part-1500/section-1500.18). Use a soft ball you can see is much bigger than that hole.

Books, crayons, and similar writing materials are exempt from the small-parts ban, because they cannot be made so they never break into bits. The exemption is not a promise that a bitten crayon is safe. Stay beside a child who chews them. Household objects are not tested like toys. If your child still mouths things, keep the cylinder as the line.

Skip toys with a button battery or high-powered magnets. HealthyChildren.org lists both in its toy-safety notes, and the CPSC [magnet page](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Magnets) describes the injury risk if magnets are swallowed. Water beads stay out. The [CPSC water-bead alert](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/SafetyAlert_5101_WaterBeads_082024.pdf) says to remove them from places with children 3 and younger. Uninflated or broken latex balloons are a choking-hazard category in the CPSC [small-parts labeling FAQ](https://www.cpsc.gov/FAQ/Small-Parts). They do not belong on this shelf. The same hazards, written as a shop checklist, are in <Link href="/blog/safe-toys-for-toddlers">how to choose safe toys for toddlers</Link>.

Material checks for a younger baby, including edges and cleaning, stay in the infant safety post. Do not move a child onto smaller pieces because a box says 12 months.

## Hands

At this age the useful toy gives the fingers a small, repeated job: pick up a large block, drop it in a cup, stack two, then scribble once the marks start. By 12 months most children use a thumb-and-finger pinch on food. By 18 months most scribble and try a spoon. By 2 years most eat with a spoon. The household versions of those jobs, with cups and paper, are in <Link href="/blog/fine-motor-activities-toddlers">fine motor activities for toddlers</Link>.

**Look for:** a wide cup, blocks you can see across the room, a short chunky crayon once they are marking paper. NAEYC suggests wide washable crayons and large paper for 1-year-olds. You still sit with them.

**Skip:** beads, coins, puzzle knobs that unscrew, and any “busy” board with pieces that come off in a small hand. Stringing can wait. The CDC’s “large beads” line is on the [3-year checklist](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/3-years.html), not this one.

## Movement

The first movement toy is often a reason to pull up and walk, not a reason to sit. The CDC [1-year tips](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/1-year.html) say to let a baby push empty boxes, a small chair, or push toys, and they say baby walkers are not recommended. A push toy means the child stands and walks behind it. A seat-in walker is a different object. Keep the path clear of stairs.

By 18 months most children walk without holding on and climb on and off a couch or chair. By 2 years most kick a ball and run. The World Health Organization’s [2019 guidelines](https://www.who.int/news-room/detail/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more) say children ages 1 to 2 should spend at least 180 minutes a day in physical activity of any intensity, spread through the day. Walking to the table counts. One toy does not have to fill it. Rainy-day floor games are in <Link href="/blog/indoor-gross-motor-activities-toddlers">indoor gross motor activities</Link>.

**Look for:** a stable push toy with a wide base, a soft ball much larger than the 1.75-inch hole, a clear patch of floor.

**Skip:** seat-in baby walkers, trampolines, ride-ons aimed at a doorway or stairs, and any ball small enough to pass the hole test. Balloons stay out.

## First words and books

A 1-year-old’s language toy is usually a book or an object you can name. You still supply the sentence. The CDC 1-year tips say reading can be talking about pictures, and that babies this age like books they can feel or flaps they can lift. By 15 months most children try one or two words besides mama or dada and look at a familiar object when you name it. By 18 months most try three or more words besides mama or dada and look at a few pages with you. By 2 years most point to a picture when you ask “where is the bear?” and say at least two words together.

How to read the book is its own skill, in <Link href="/blog/reading-aloud-language-development">reading aloud</Link>. Letter drills can wait. They fit better near ages 4 and 5, in <Link href="/blog/reading-readiness-activities-preschoolers">reading readiness activities</Link>.

Electronic toys are a weak language buy at this age. In a study of 26 parent-infant pairs, infants 10 to 16 months old, play with electronic toys came with fewer adult words, fewer back-and-forth turns, and fewer parent responses than play with books or traditional toys ([Sosa, JAMA Pediatrics, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26720437/)). HealthyChildren.org says digital toys should not replace face-to-face play, and the CDC milestone pages say screen time is not recommended for children younger than 2. The screen post is <Link href="/blog/balancing-screen-time">balancing screen time</Link>. This page stays with the book and the doll.

**Look for:** a sturdy board book, a doll or animal you can name, flaps that are still attached. If a flap tears off and fits in the cylinder, that book is done.

**Skip:** a toy that speaks every line so the child never has to try a word, and loose magnetic letters. Learning toys for a 12 month old are a book and a cup you talk about, not a kit with a lesson.

## Pretend play and cause and effect

Pretend at this age is short and copied. By 15 months most children try to use a phone, a cup, or a book the way it is used, and most hug a stuffed doll. By 18 months most play with a toy in a simple way, such as pushing a car, and most copy a chore such as sweeping. The CDC 18-month tips suggest a spoon so the child can pretend to feed a stuffed animal, and a doll with a blanket. By 2 years most children play with more than one toy at the same time.

That is the start of the play described in <Link href="/blog/pretend-play-social-skills">pretend play</Link> and <Link href="/blog/social-emotional-learning">social-emotional toys</Link>. It is not yet a long game with friends. NAEYC notes that 1-year-olds often play beside other children, not with them in a shared plan. Turn-taking sets can wait. They are covered later in <Link href="/blog/cooperative-play-toys-sharing">cooperative play toys</Link>.

Cause and effect is the same shelf when the child does the action. By 2 years most children try switches, knobs, or buttons on a toy. A large knob that opens a door, or a block that disappears into a cup, is enough. The CDC 1-year tips also suggest pots and pans, or a simple drum. Skip cymbals and any mallet that fits in the cylinder. How to choose later instruments, and what not to put in a shaker, is in <Link href="/blog/musical-milestones-music-toys">music toys</Link>.

**Look for:** one doll with embroidered features, one sturdy car, one pot and a wooden spoon, one large button or knob that the child presses.

**Skip:** a 40-piece pretend set, toys with eyes that unscrew, and anything that plays a whole song so the child only watches.

## Sorting and stacking

By 12 months most children put something in a container. By 15 months most stack at least two objects. By 2 years most hold one thing while the other hand takes a lid off. Filling, dumping, and stacking two are the game. A tower of ten can wait.

NAEYC’s 1-year-old list includes cardboard and wood blocks that can be smaller than infant blocks, about 2 to 4 inches. A block that still fits in the small-parts cylinder is too small, even if it is in that range. A shape sorter belongs here only when every piece is too big for the cylinder and the child can still get it out.

When they are closer to 2 and you are not buying anything, the household jobs are in <Link href="/blog/montessori-activities-2-year-olds">Montessori-inspired activities for 2-year-olds</Link>. That post is about 24 to 36 months. Questions such as “which tower has more?” fit better from about age 3, in <Link href="/blog/preschool-stem-activities-at-home">preschool STEM activities</Link>.

**Look for:** three or four cups, a few blocks, a sorter with a handful of large shapes. You should be able to put half of it away and still have a game.

**Skip:** marble runs, building bricks the size of a fingertip, and puzzles with many small pieces. Rice, beans, and water beads are not sorting toys. What to leave out of a sensory bin is in <Link href="/blog/sensory-play-activities-toddlers">sensory play for toddlers</Link> and <Link href="/blog/diy-sensory-bottles-toddlers">DIY sensory bottles</Link>.

## Toy ideas by type

These are types, not products. No brand, price, or rating belongs in this list. Use it later as a slot for a specific pick, after the piece-size check above.

| Toy type | Skill it invites | Piece-size note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stacking or nesting cups | Fill, dump, stack two | Cups too big for the cylinder |
| Large blocks, about 2 to 4 inches | Stack, carry, pretend | Nothing that fits in the cylinder |
| Shape sorter | Post a shape, take it out | Every piece too big to swallow |
| Push toy the child walks behind | Pull up, cruise, walk | Not a seat-in baby walker |
| Soft ball | Roll, then kick | Must not pass a 1.75-inch hole |
| Board book | Point, name, turn a page | Retire it if a flap tears off |
| Stuffed doll or soft animal | Hug, pretend to feed | Embroidered features, no loose eyes |
| Sturdy car or animal figure | Push, name | One piece, or parts that stay on |
| Pot and wooden spoon | Bang, cause and effect | Spoon handle too big for the cylinder |
| Chunky puzzle, a few pieces | Place one piece | Knob and piece both too big to swallow |
| Toy with one large knob or button | Try a switch | No battery door they can open |

A cup from the kitchen can stand in for the first row. A pot can stand in for the drum. Buy when that type is missing, cracked, or too small, not because the table has an empty cell.

## How many to have, and when to buy

There is no official correct number of toys for a 1-year-old. One study of 36 toddlers compared supervised free play with 4 toys and with 16 toys. With fewer toys, children played longer with a single toy and used toys in more ways ([Dauch and colleagues, Infant Behavior and Development, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29190457/)). That was one session, not a rule for a whole cupboard. It is a reason to put out a few toys, not every toy.

HealthyChildren.org says a certain toy is not necessary for a child to reach the next milestone. Buy when a skill you see this month has no material at all, or when the pieces they have are cracked, too small, or too frustrating to repeat. Start with what is in the house: a book, a ball, a cup, a doll, and a pot. The activity posts linked above are the no-purchase versions.

The useful question for toys for 1 year old development is which skill they repeat this week, not how many boxes are labeled educational.

Check the maker’s age label, then check the child. A label of 12 months or 18 months means the product was graded for that age, including small-parts rules for children under 3. It does not mean this child is done putting things in their mouth.

## Frequently asked questions

### What educational toys does a 1-year-old need?

A few they will use: something to drop into or stack, something to push or roll, a board book, and a doll or soft animal. Match the toy to the skill you see this month. The CDC lists are a reference for what most children do, not a cart.

### Are electronic learning toys a good buy at 12 months?

Usually no, when the goal is words. In the Sosa study, infants 10 to 16 months heard fewer adult words and had fewer back-and-forth turns during electronic-toy play than during books or traditional toys. A single large button the child presses can still be a cause-and-effect toy. A toy that talks through the whole game leaves them less to say.

### What if my 1-year-old still mouths toys?

Keep the under-3 size rule. Large pieces only. No water beads, no beads, no loose magnets, no balloons, no button batteries. A 2-year-old who still mouths toys stays on this shelf, not the age-3 one.

### Do I need a separate toy for each skill?

No. Cups can be a stack, a dump, and a pretend drink. A doll can be a hug and a reason to talk. The table is a way to notice a gap, not a requirement to own five categories.

### How is this different from the main toy guide and the age-3 page?

The main guide covers babies through school age and the general buying test. The age-3 page is hands, movement, words, pretend play, and building for 3-year-olds, including larger beads and simple turn-taking. This page is only about 12 to 24 months, with the piece-size rule for children who still mouth toys.

### What are learning toys for a 12 month old who is not walking yet?

A cup to fill, a board book, and something steady to pull up on or push, such as a push toy used on a clear floor. On the CDC lists, pulling up and walking while holding furniture are 12-month lines. A few steps on their own is the 15-month line. Walking without holding on is the 18-month line.

## What to try first

Watch one play session. Write down the skill they repeat: dropping a block in a cup, pulling up, hugging a doll, or turning pages. Get or pull out one toy for that skill. Leave the other rows for another week.

Then read the matching activity post linked above if you would rather not buy, and the <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-child-development">educational toys guide</Link> if you want the full safety check. If they are closer to 3 than to 1, use the <Link href="/blog/educational-toys-for-3-year-olds">age-3 page</Link> instead.
